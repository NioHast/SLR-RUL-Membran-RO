# Mechanism-aligned machine learning for membrane fouling prediction and process control in pressure-driven water treatment membranes: A review

Xin Yang , Jundong Zhao , Junzhao Zhuang , Songlin Guan , Chunbo Wang , Kenglin Huang , Lele Liu , Yunan Gao \*

*School of Environmental and Chemical Engineering, Foshan University, Foshan, China*

#### ARTICLE INFO

Editor: Amir Razmjou

*Keywords:* Membrane fouling Machine learning Explainable artificial intelligence Process control Water reuse

#### ABSTRACT

Pressure-driven membrane processes are essential for water reclamation, drinking-water treatment, industrial wastewater reuse and resource recovery. However, their long-term stability is limited by membrane fouling, which is governed by hydraulic history, influent variability, organic-inorganic interactions, biofilm growth and localized scaling. This review develops a mechanism-aligned machine-learning framework for fouling prediction and process control in ultrafiltration, nanofiltration and reverse osmosis systems. The framework links dominant fouling mechanisms with data structures, model architectures and practical control actions. Gated recurrent unit models are discussed for ultrafiltration systems with high-frequency hydraulic signals and rapid cake-layer development, where they can support adaptive flux regulation, air-scouring adjustment and backwash scheduling. Regularized XGBoost models are evaluated for nanofiltration systems with multicollinear water-quality variables and coupled organic-inorganic fouling, where they can guide pretreatment optimization, coagulant dosing and chemical-dose reduction. Hybrid convolutional neural network-gated recurrent unit models combined with SHAP are examined for reverse osmosis systems with spatially heterogeneous biofouling and scaling, where they can support early-warning diagnosis, pH correction, antiscalant control, targeted cleaning and biocide zoning. The review also discusses benchmarking, SCADA noise, data sparsity, fallback control, edge deployment and operator interpretability. Future work should prioritize plant-scale validation, physics-informed learning, digital twins and federated learning to support safer and more deployable artificial intelligence in water-treatment facilities.

#### **1. Introduction**

Driven by water scarcity, stricter discharge limits and the need for fitfor-purpose reuse, pressure-driven membrane technologies have become important unit operations in modern water process engineering [\[1\]](#page-15-0). These processes form a complementary separation hierarchy for complex water matrices [\[2\].](#page-15-0) Ultrafiltration (UF) is widely used in municipal wastewater reclamation and drinking-water pretreatment. Nanofiltration (NF) supports selective ion separation, organic-matter control and resource recovery from industrial streams. Reverse osmosis (RO) provides a dense separation barrier for high-salinity wastewater reuse, brackish-water treatment and polishing steps in advanced treatment trains. Despite these advantages, stable long-term operation remains limited by membrane fouling. Fouling shortens membrane service life, increases cleaning frequency, raises specific energy consumption (SEC) and can reduce the reliability of water-treatment facilities [\[3\]](#page-15-0).

Importantly, membrane performance decline is not a singular or static event. Instead, it is a highly complex, non-linear and spatiotemporal phenomenon [\[4\].](#page-15-0) This process is driven by the synergistic interactions among physical pore blockage, organic adsorption, inorganic scaling and microbial degradation [\[5\]](#page-15-0). Across the membrane separation hierarchy, the dominant fouling mechanisms differ substantially in both physical characteristics and temporal evolution. This pronounced heterogeneity makes conventional fouling mitigation strategies fundamentally inadequate. These strategies mainly rely on deterministic mathematical models, such as classical Hermia blocking laws and solution-diffusion derivations [\[6\]](#page-15-0). They also depend on static influent water quality indicators, such as the Silt Density Index (SDI). In addition, reactive clean-in-place (CIP) protocols are typically initiated only after severe flux decline has already occurred [\[7\].](#page-15-0) Full-scale membrane operations often face dynamic and high-frequency influent fluctuations. Under such conditions, traditional experience-based heuristics cannot effectively decouple complex cross-correlations. Consequently, membrane systems often suffer from suboptimal hydraulic control, excessive chemical consumption and accelerated irreversible degradation of polyamide active layers [\[8\].](#page-15-0)

To overcome the limits of deterministic modeling, computational tools have been increasingly used in membrane engineering. This trend is moving the field from empirical approximation toward data-assisted process control [\[9\].](#page-15-0) Advanced algorithms can extract non-linear patterns from heterogeneous operational datasets. They can also support dynamic hydraulic optimization and early-warning diagnostics [\[10\]](#page-15-0). However, a clear analytical gap remains. Many studies apply neural networks or ensemble trees across membrane systems without sufficient mechanistic distinction. They often do not explain why a given model is appropriate for UF, NF, or RO fouling [\[11\].](#page-15-0) Broad membrane-technology reviews have summarized sustainable water-treatment applications and general performance limitations, including fouling, but have only limited or indirect discussion of ML-enabled control [\[12\]](#page-15-0). Reviews focused on dynamic fouling characterization have clarified the spatial and temporal evolution of deposits in pressure-driven systems, whereas AI model selection and operational decision translation are not their central objectives [\[13\].](#page-15-0) AI-oriented reviews have moved the field closer to data-driven fouling prediction, but their comparison is often organized around algorithm categories or prediction accuracy. For example, AI-based fouling-prediction reviews emphasize ANN and related models while treating fouling factors mainly as input variables [\[14\].](#page-15-0) Reviews of ML for membrane-separation performance in wastewater treatment summarize common algorithms and performance indicators, but fouling is usually one of several process issues rather than the organizing mechanism [\[15\]](#page-15-0). Process-specific reviews on RO and NF provide deeper discussion of scaling, biofouling, separation performance and AI/ML applications, but they remain largely confined to one membrane level and do not systematically connect UF, NF and RO fouling pathways with different model-control requirements [\[16,17\].](#page-15-0)

A stronger framework is therefore needed. It should align each mathematical architecture with the relevant physicochemical boundary conditions. It should also provide explanations that operators can use before changing hydraulic or chemical setpoints. To address this gap, this review develops a mechanism-driven framework for membrane fouling prediction and process control. The framework links predictive accuracy with physical interpretability and operational usefulness. It first clarifies representative application scenarios of UF, NF and RO systems [\[18\]](#page-16-0). It then compares the dominant fouling mechanisms across these separation levels and explains how these mechanisms shape data structures and control objectives. On this basis, the review evaluates three representative model families. Gated recurrent unit (GRU) models are discussed for rapid UF cake-layer development [\[19\].](#page-16-0) XGBoost is discussed for NF systems with coupled organic-inorganic water-quality variables [\[20\].](#page-16-0) Hybrid CNN-GRU models are discussed for RO systems with spatially heterogeneous biofouling and scaling [\[21\].](#page-16-0) This article is a narrative and critical review rather than a formal meta-analysis. It does not pool effect sizes across studies. Instead, it organizes the literature by membrane process, dominant fouling mechanism, data type, validation approach and control objective [\[22\].](#page-16-0) Four questions guide the analysis: which fouling mechanism dominates the process? which data structure is available? which model architecture fits that structure? and which operational decision the model can support? This logic helps avoid the generic use of black-box algorithms and makes the engineering value of each model easier to evaluate [\[23\]](#page-16-0). Finally, the review examines explainable artificial intelligence (XAI), fallback control and edge deployment as requirements for safer use of AI in digital-twin-enabled water infrastructure.

#### **2. Literature search and selection methodology**

This review was designed as a critical narrative review with a transparent and reproducible literature-selection procedure rather than as a quantitative meta-analysis. The literature search covered peerreviewed articles, reviews and selected methodological papers published from January 2010; earlier landmark studies were retained only when they were needed to define classical fouling mechanisms, membrane-process terminology or model-theory foundations. The search was conducted in Scopus, Web of Science Core Collection, ScienceDirect, Google Scholar, IEEE Xplore and PubMed. Search strings combined three term groups: membrane processes ("ultrafiltration",

"nanofiltration", "reverse osmosis" and "pressure-driven membrane"), fouling mechanisms ("membrane fouling", "cake layer", "pore blocking", "organic fouling", "biofouling" and "scaling") and data-driven control methods ("machine learning", "deep learning", "XGBoost", "GRU", "CNN", "SHAP", "explainable artificial intelligence", "process control" and "digital twin"). Backward and forward citation tracking was then performed for highly cited membrane-fouling reviews, recent AI-assisted membrane studies and key papers on interpretable machine learning.

The initial database search identified 1822 records: Scopus (*n* = 548), Web of Science Core Collection (*n* = 462), ScienceDirect (*n* = 344), Google Scholar (first 250 relevance-ranked records), IEEE Xplore (*n* = 118) and PubMed (*n* = 100). Citation tracking added 41 records, giving 1863 records before deduplication. After 502 duplicate records were removed, 1361 records remained for title and abstract screening. At this stage, 877 records were excluded because they were outside pressuredriven membrane systems, did not address fouling, lacked a datadriven or control-relevant component, or focused only on material synthesis without operational interpretation. The remaining 484 fulltext articles were assessed in detail. Of these, 210 were excluded because they lacked sufficient process conditions, did not report foulingor control-related outputs, provided no model-validation information, were not relevant to UF/NF/RO water-treatment operation, or substantially overlapped with a more complete later publication. Finally, 274 publications were retained and cited in the review.

Studies were included when they met at least one of the following criteria: (i) they examined fouling formation, fouling diagnosis, flux decline, permeability loss, transmembrane-pressure evolution, saltrejection deterioration or cleaning response in UF, NF or RO systems; (ii) they used machine-learning, deep-learning, hybrid mechanisticdata-driven, explainable-AI or control-oriented modeling approaches relevant to membrane operation; (iii) they reported operating variables, sensor signals, water-quality descriptors, imaging or spectroscopic data that could be linked to fouling signatures; or (iv) they provided methodological foundations for model validation, uncertainty handling, feature attribution, deployment latency, edge implementation or safe process control.

Studies were excluded when they focused exclusively on nonpressure-driven separation, lacked a water-treatment or membraneoperation context, reported only qualitative claims without usable technical detail, duplicated information already covered by a more complete source, or could not be connected to the mechanism-to-control framework developed in this review. Because the aim was to build a mechanism-aligned synthesis, the final analysis prioritized evidence that explicitly linked fouling mechanisms, measurable data signatures, model outputs and actionable operational decisions.

## **3. Fouling mechanisms and data signatures in pressure-driven membrane processes**

To deploy predictive algorithms in water-treatment facilities, the model architecture should reflect both the physical fouling process and the available operational data. This section therefore examines the engineering context of pressure-driven membrane technologies. It identifies the main physicochemical failure points, the associated data signatures and the control actions that are realistic for each separation level.

#### *3.1. Application scope and process-control value*

Pressure-driven membrane technologies are used across a wide range of water-treatment, wastewater-reuse and resource-recovery scenarios. Their engineering value is shaped by influent composition, regulatory targets, hydraulic constraints and available plant infrastructure. UF is widely used as a robust barrier for suspended solids, colloids and pathogens in drinking-water production and wastewater reclamation.

NF is often applied where selective ion separation, organic-matter control or resource recovery is required. RO contains a compact polyamide active layer and provides the densest pressure-driven separation barrier for high-salinity wastewater reuse, brackish-water treatment and advanced polishing. Table 1 summarizes quantitative separation metrics, operating ranges, warning thresholds, sampling intervals and deployment-related model metrics; qualitative input variables and mechanism-control interpretations are discussed below.

As shown in Table 1, UF, NF and RO require different model-control pairings because their measurable data streams and operational risks are not identical. UF models are usually driven by high-frequency transmembrane pressure (TMP), flux, turbidity, particle-count, temperature and backwash-history records. These signals reflect rapid particle deposition, colloid accumulation and cake-layer compaction under shock loads, so sequence models or anomaly-detection modules can support flux correction, air-scouring adjustment and backwash scheduling [\[43\]](#page-16-0). NF models are usually built from pressure, flux, conductivity, DOC, UV254, pH and dosing records. These variables describe steric exclusion, electrostatic effects and dissolved organic matter (DOM) metal interactions, so regularized tree ensembles can support coagulant, pH and antiscalant-dose optimization [\[44\].](#page-16-0) RO models combine pressure, flux, conductivity, pH and antiscalant records with optical coherence tomography (OCT) or hyperspectral fouling images when available; because localized biofouling, scaling and chemical-cleaning stress are strongly spatiotemporal, multimodal or hybrid models are more suitable for early-warning, targeted chemical dosing and CIPtiming decisions [\[45\]](#page-16-0). Thus, the key issue for AI-assisted process control is not to rank these processes as good or poor, but to match each process with the data streams, fouling mechanisms, validation methods and intervention strategies that are most relevant to its engineering role.

## *3.2.1. UF: Dynamic cake-layer formation*

Fig. 1a illustrates the sequential evolution of UF fouling from initial pore blocking to cake-layer compaction. At the initial stage, physical pore interception is the dominant fouling mechanism. This stage is soon followed by the formation of a macroscopic cake layer [\[49\]](#page-16-0). The whole process is highly dynamic and is accompanied by high-frequency hydraulic fluctuations. For example, algae-laden surface water or stormwater-impacted municipal wastewater can cause rapid particle deposition [\[50\]](#page-16-0). These deposited materials include colloidal particles, EPS and macromolecular organics. As a result, fouling develops quickly and triggers a rapid increase in TMP. This increase can occur within short operational windows [\[51\]](#page-16-0). The deterioration may progress from reversible pore shielding to irreversible cake-layer compaction. Eventually, a composite fouling architecture is formed. Fundamentally, this process is controlled by hydrodynamic shear forces and localized concentration polarization [\[52\].](#page-16-0)

## *3.2.2. NF: DOM adsorption and donnan shielding*

In NF systems, as shown in Fig. 1b, the fouling trajectory is shaped by

several interacting mechanisms. These include steric hindrance, hydrophobic affinity, and Donnan electrostatic interactions. DOM plays a key role in initiating interfacial resistance. More specifically, lowmolecular-weight protein-like substances are major contributors [\[53\]](#page-16-0). Medium-to-high-molecular-weight humic compounds also act as important fouling instigators. These specific DOM fractions are especially influential. They account for up to 90.17% of the variance in NF fouling trajectories [\[54\].](#page-16-0)

The coexistence of multivalent metal cations, such as *Ca*<sup>2</sup><sup>+</sup> and *Mg*<sup>2</sup><sup>+</sup>, with humic substances creates a serious engineering challenge [\[55\]](#page-16-0). The Donnan Steric Pore Model with Dielectric Exclusion (DSPM-DE) can help explain this interaction at the molecular scale [\[56\]](#page-16-0). In this framework, multivalent cations cross-link with specific natural organic matter (NOM) fractions. This process changes the effective volumetric charge density (*Xd*) in the nanopores. It may also alter the local dielectric constant (*ϵp*) [\[57\]](#page-16-0). These organic-inorganic complexes can mask the negative functional groups on the polyamide surface. Carboxylate groups are especially important. In some cases, charge inversion may occur.

This molecular-level charge shielding severely weakens the Donnan potential at the membrane-solution interface. As a result, electrostatic repulsion fails prematurely [\[58\]](#page-16-0). At the macroscopic scale, this microscopic interaction causes a highly non-linear and asymptotic decline in permeability. At the same time, multivalent ion selectivity undergoes a systemic collapse. The gelation process is driven by strong synergistic interactions. These interactions generate highly multidimensional operational datasets with extreme multicollinearity. This problem is reflected in the strongly overlapping variance patterns among fluctuating pH, divalent cation concentrations, and UV254 absorbance [\[59\]](#page-16-0). This complex and strongly coupled data matrix creates major challenges for traditional deterministic models. Therefore, regularized highdimensional gradient boosting frameworks, such as XGBoost, are needed. These frameworks can decouple the feature spaces and identify the primary fouling vectors [\[60\]](#page-16-0).

## *3.2.3. RO: Scaling and biofouling*

RO membranes contain compact polyamide active layers and therefore provide the highest-density pressure-driven separation barrier [\[61\]](#page-16-0). This structural advantage supports high salt rejection, but it also makes long-term operation sensitive to inorganic scaling and biological degradation [\[62\]](#page-16-0). [Fig. 1](#page-3-0)c highlights the spatiotemporal heterogeneity of RO fouling. In these systems, different failure modes may appear in localized micro-environments.

During brackish-water desalination or high-salinity industrial wastewater treatment, supersaturated ions often crystallize on the membrane surface [\[63\].](#page-16-0) These crystals then form inorganic scales. Dense gypsum scales formed by calcium and sulfate are a typical example. This pathway corresponds to the scaling zone in [Fig. 1.](#page-3-0) Such crystallization can account for more than 90% of the total inorganic fouling mass [\[64\].](#page-16-0) At the same time, localized biofouling creates an active and continuously growing threat. Initial biofilm colonization strongly depends on the physicochemical properties of the conditioning layer. Its polarity and acid-base free energy are particularly important [\[65\]](#page-16-0). After initial bacterial attachment, microorganisms secrete a complex matrix of EPS. This matrix mainly consists of highly hydrated polysaccharides, structural proteins, and extracellular DNA (eDNA) [\[66\]](#page-16-0).

This EPS assembly forms a strong three-dimensional gel scaffold. It acts as both a physical and biochemical shield. It slows the diffusion of standard CIP agents, such as sodium hypochlorite and non-oxidizing biocides. It can also reduce their toxicity. As a result, the bacterial community becomes more resistant to environmental stressors [\[67\]](#page-16-0). Consequently, biofilm formation is highly insidious and non-linear. It is also marked by strong spatiotemporal heterogeneity [\[68\].](#page-17-0) When conventional supervisory control and data acquisition (SCADA) sensors detect increased TMP or reduced permeate flow, the biofilm is usually already mature. At this stage, the biofilm is encased in EPS and becomes highly recalcitrant. Therefore, reactive CIP protocols are often ineffective.

To overcome this limitation, biofilm evolution must be monitored at the nascent stage. Localized EPS matrix densification can produce distinct optical refractive-index variations [\[69\].](#page-17-0) Dynamic changes in the hydration layer can also generate morphological thickness microtextures. These features can be detected by non-invasive optical monitoring techniques, including OCT and hyperspectral imaging [\[70\]](#page-17-0). These complex biological transformations can be mapped into high-resolution spatial feature tensors. Based on these tensors, convolutional neural networks (CNN) can quantitatively extract subtle optical perturbations through multidimensional convolutional kernels [\[71\].](#page-17-0) By directly linking physical microbiology with computer-vision features, advanced predictive models can function as digital microscopes for early biofouling diagnosis [\[72\].](#page-17-0) This approach provides a rigorous scientific basis for diagnosing and intercepting early biofouling. It also allows intervention before irreversible macroscopic hydraulic deterioration occurs [\[73\]](#page-17-0).

For non-invasive RO fouling monitoring, OCT and hyperspectral imaging should be treated as complementary diagnostic tools rather than as equivalent sensors [\[74\]](#page-17-0). OCT is mainly suited to depth-resolved structural diagnosis because it can reconstruct cross-sectional or threedimensional information on fouling-layer thickness, biofilm architecture and cake or scale morphology. However, its field of view is limited, optical access to the membrane surface is required and signal quality may decline in highly turbid feed channels or modules without optical windows [\[75\].](#page-17-0) Hyperspectral imaging is more suitable for larger-area chemically informative screening because wavelength-dependent

spectral signatures can reflect organic matter, biofilm pigments, hydration-state changes and some mineral deposits. However, it produces high-dimensional spectral cubes that require stable illumination, calibration, feature compression and data-storage capacity [\[76\]](#page-17-0). Therefore, OCT is better positioned for pilot-scale studies, sentinel modules or selected high-risk RO trains, whereas hyperspectral imaging is more appropriate for offline or near-line diagnosis and targeted modules coupled with edge preprocessing before CNN-GRU modeling [\[77\]](#page-17-0).

## *3.3. Limits of deterministic models and need for data-driven control*

Conventional fouling mitigation strategies rely heavily on static influent water-quality guidelines, such as SDI thresholds. They also depend on deterministic mass-transfer equations. In addition, passive chemical cleaning is often used. However, this cleaning is usually triggered only after severe flux decline has already occurred [\[78\].](#page-17-0) These empirical heuristics are no longer sufficient because they cannot fully address the dynamic realities of modern water-treatment plants.

For example, traditional engineering guidelines often assume that high volumetric recovery rates directly lead to high fouling risks. However, this assumption is too simplistic. It may unnecessarily limit system water recovery efficiency [\[79\].](#page-17-0) Conventional chemical interventions can also create secondary problems. Low concentrations of non-oxidizing biocides, such as methylisothiazolinone (MIT), may activate quorum sensing pathways in disinfectant-resistant bacteria [\[80\]](#page-17-0). This activation can promote aggressive biofilm formation. It may also increase biomass by up to 180% [\[81\]](#page-17-0). At a fundamental level, deterministic mathematical models struggle to resolve deep non-linear crosscorrelations. These correlations involve fluctuating pH, temperature, complex DOM fractions and hydrodynamic shear forces. Their complexity becomes even more pronounced during prolonged operational cycles [\[82\].](#page-17-0)

AI technologies offer strong potential for addressing this industrywide bottleneck. However, the blind application of generic algorithms is unlikely to succeed. The physical mechanistic differences discussed above must be properly considered [\[83\].](#page-17-0) Different fouling mechanisms require different AI capabilities. Macroscopic cake-layer accumulation requires rapid temporal response [\[84\].](#page-17-0) Charge masking in gel layers involves extreme multicollinearity. It therefore requires highdimensional feature decoupling [\[85\].](#page-17-0) Localized biofilm outbreaks require multimodal spatial processing. This processing is needed to capture their extreme spatiotemporal evolution [\[86\].](#page-17-0) The following sections establish a unified framework. This framework aims to align frontier predictive architectures with distinct physical mechanisms in a precise mathematical manner.

## **4. Predictive architectures and mechanistic alignment**

Across the pressure-driven membrane hierarchy, fouling prediction requires models that are consistent with the underlying process dynamics. A useful model should not only improve statistical accuracy, but also support a feasible and bounded control action, such as adaptive backwashing, pretreatment-dose adjustment, pH correction, antiscalant control or targeted cleaning [\[87\]](#page-17-0). To make this logic clearer, [Fig. 2](#page-5-0) summarizes the mechanism-to-control pathway rather than only presenting a detailed algorithmic illustration. The figure follows five sequential layers: dominant fouling mechanism, measurable data signature, model selection, operator-facing model output and practical control action.

As illustrated in [Fig. 2,](#page-5-0) the mechanism-aligned framework should be interpreted as a control-oriented decision pathway rather than a simple comparison of model architectures. The pathway begins with the dominant fouling mechanism, converts this mechanism into measurable data signatures, selects a model according to the available data structure, and finally translates the model output into bounded operational

For UF, rapid pore blocking and cake-layer compaction generate TMP slope, flux decline, turbidity and backwash-history signals, which can be processed by GRU models to predict TMP acceleration and guide flux regulation, air scouring and backwash scheduling [\[88\]](#page-17-0). For NF, DOM adsorption, cation bridging and charge shielding generate multicollinear water-quality and permeability signals, which can be analyzed by regularized XGBoost to identify key fouling drivers and support coagulant dosing, pH optimization and recovery adjustment [\[89\]](#page-17-0). For RO, localized scaling and biofouling generate differential-pressure, salt-passage, pH, recovery and imagingbased signatures, which can be integrated by CNN-GRU-SHAP models to provide spatial risk diagnosis, SHAP-based explanations, antiscalant or pH correction, targeted cleaning, biocide zoning and module inspection [\[90\].](#page-17-0) All actions should be constrained by plant-defined limits, membrane-manufacturer recommendations and operator override.

#### *4.1. Practical interpretation and implementation of the mechanismaligned framework*

The UF–GRU, NF–XGBoost and RO–CNN–GRU–SHAP links proposed in this review should be interpreted as representative guidelines rather than fixed model-allocation rules. In practical membrane plants, model selection depends on data availability, sampling frequency, sensor modality, dataset size, prediction horizon and the intended control objective [\[91\].](#page-17-0) For example, XGBoost can also be highly useful for RO plant datasets when the available inputs are structured SCADA variables, such as pressure, flow rate, recovery, conductivity, pH, temperature, antiscalant dose and normalized salt passage. Conversely, recurrent models can be relevant to NF systems when the target is timedependent permeability loss, salt-rejection drift or cleaning demand under high-frequency operation. Similarly, CNN-based components are justified only when image, OCT, hyperspectral or spatially resolved sensor data are available [\[93\].](#page-17-0) Therefore, the proposed framework should be used as a decision pathway. The fouling signature and data structure should be identified first, and the simplest model that satisfies the prediction and control requirement should then be selected.

To make the discussion of fouling mechanisms more operational, each mechanism should be linked to measurable indicators. In UF systems, cake-layer formation and compaction can be tracked using TMP increase rate, normalized flux decline, permeability loss, turbidity, particle counts, backwash interval, air-scouring demand and CIP frequency [\[94\].](#page-17-0) In NF systems, organic-inorganic fouling should be connected with normalized permeability, rejection drift, DOC, UV254, fluorescence indices, pH, ionic strength and *Ca*<sup>2</sup><sup>+</sup>/*Mg*<sup>2</sup><sup>+</sup> concentrations. These variables reflect DOM adsorption, cation bridging and charge shielding [\[95\]](#page-17-0). In RO systems, scaling and biofouling should be evaluated using differential pressure, normalized permeate flow, normalized salt passage, conductivity, pH, temperature, recovery, antiscalant dose, calcium or sulfate saturation tendency, ATP/EPS indicators when available, cleaning frequency and specific energy consumption [\[96\]](#page-17-0). The prediction target should therefore be defined as a plant-relevant endpoint. Examples include TMP increase over a future filtration cycle, percentage permeability loss, probability of crossing a cleaning threshold, expected time to CIP, or recommended dosing, flux or backwash adjustment [\[97\]](#page-17-0).

Before any model is linked to control, an implementation workflow is required. Raw plant data should first be synchronized by timestamp and separated into filtration, backwash, chemical-cleaning, shutdown and abnormal-operation periods [\[98\].](#page-17-0) Physically impossible readings should <span id="page-6-0"></span>be removed or flagged according to engineering limits [\[99\].](#page-17-0) Noisy hydraulic signals may be treated using moving-median, low-pass or Kalman-type filters [\[100\]](#page-17-0). However, the filtering window should remain shorter than the expected early-warning horizon so that rapid TMP increases or flux-loss signals are not erased. Missing data should be handled according to gap length and physical plausibility. Short gaps may be filled by interpolation, K-nearest neighbor (KNN) or modelbased reconstruction [\[101\].](#page-17-0) Long gaps should trigger missingness flags, sample exclusion or fallback to a more conservative model [\[102\]](#page-17-0). When possible, variables should be normalized using temperaturecorrected permeability, normalized flux and normalized pressure, because raw values may mix true fouling effects with changes in operational setpoints.

Feature construction and validation should also be process-specific. For tabular XGBoost models, candidate inputs should include waterquality variables, hydraulic variables, chemical-dosing records and lagged or rolling descriptors of recent operation [\[103\]](#page-17-0). Feature selection can combine engineering judgement, permutation importance and SHAP analysis, while recognizing that feature importance does not by itself prove causality [\[104\]](#page-17-0). For GRU or CNN-GRU models, sequence length, prediction horizon and sampling frequency should be selected according to filtration-cycle duration and the required control lead time. Random train splitting should be avoided for temporally correlated plant data. A more defensible strategy is chronological splitting, rollingorigin validation, or independent validation across seasons, influent regimes or membrane modules. Hyperparameters should be tuned only

on the validation set, including GRU hidden units, dropout and sequence window [\[105\];](#page-17-0) XGBoost tree depth, learning rate, number of trees and L1/L2 penalties [\[106\]](#page-17-0); and CNN kernel size, filter number and compression ratio [\[107\].](#page-17-0) Model uncertainty should be reported using ensemble predictions, quantile regression or confidence intervals [\[108\]](#page-17-0). Finally, model drift should be monitored through residual trends, inputdistribution shifts and changes in fouling-response relationships. When drift exceeds a plant-defined tolerance, the model should be recalibrated or retrained before it is allowed to support automatic control [\[109\]](#page-17-0). To clarify how these requirements can be implemented in practice, Fig. 3 summarizes a plant-level deployment workflow from data acquisition to safeguarded control and feedback-based retraining.

Fig. 3 further translates the mechanism-aligned modeling concept into a plant-level deployment workflow for membrane-fouling prediction and process control. The workflow starts from heterogeneous plant data streams, including SCADA records, water-quality measurements, OCT or hyperspectral images and cleaning-history information. These raw inputs are first processed through data curation steps, including timestamp synchronization, anomaly flagging, filtering, missing-data handling and normalization. The curated data are then transformed into mechanism-relevant features, such as hydraulic trends, waterquality descriptors, imaging signatures, rolling statistics and cleaningrecovery indicators. This step is important because model predictions are operationally meaningful only when true fouling evolution is separated from sensor noise, operational switching and cleaning-related disturbances.

This interpretation is more consistent with full-scale membrane operation, where the best model is determined by instrumentation, data quality, control risk and operator trust.

#### *4.2. Translating model outputs into practical process-control actions*

For AI-assisted membrane operation, the predicted value itself is not the final engineering product. A model output must be translated into a bounded, auditable and reversible control action [\[112\]](#page-17-0). This requirement is particularly important because membrane plants are safetycritical systems. A prediction should therefore be interpreted through three sequential steps: diagnosis, decision support and constrained actuation [\[113\].](#page-17-0) First, the model identifies the probable fouling state or the probability of crossing a plant-defined threshold [\[114\]](#page-17-0). Second, the prediction is converted into a recommended action, such as flux adjustment, backwash rescheduling, coagulant-dose correction, pH adjustment, antiscalant-dose optimization, cleaning initiation or membrane-condition inspection [\[115\]](#page-18-0). Third, the recommended action is checked against operator-defined limits, chemical-safety rules and membrane-manufacturer constraints before implementation [\[116\].](#page-18-0)

In UF systems, a GRU-based warning of rapid TMP acceleration or flux decline can be converted into hydraulic interventions rather than immediate chemical cleaning [\[117\].](#page-18-0) When the predicted TMP slope exceeds a plant-defined warning limit within the next filtration cycle, the controller may shorten the filtration interval, increase backwash frequency, adjust air-scouring intensity or temporarily reduce permeate flux [\[118\].](#page-18-0) If repeated warnings are accompanied by incomplete hydraulic recovery, chemically enhanced backwash or CIP can be scheduled [\[119\]](#page-18-0). These actions should remain constrained by plant-defined TMP and flux limits, operator override and confirmation from normalized permeability recovery and cleaning history, so that the model output is connected to preventive hydraulic control before irreversible cake-layer compaction occurs.

In NF systems, XGBoost outputs are most useful when they are linked to pretreatment and dosing decisions. If the model attributes permeability loss to elevated UV254, DOC, *Ca*<sup>2</sup><sup>+</sup>/*Mg*<sup>2</sup><sup>+</sup> concentration or unfavorable pH, the control response may involve adjusting coagulant dose, optimizing pH, reducing instantaneous recovery, or limiting polymer addition to avoid secondary membrane fouling [\[120\]](#page-18-0). If the output indicates rejection drift or molecular weight cut-off (MWCO) attenuation risk, the recommended response should be to modify pretreatment and intensify ion-rejection monitoring rather than to apply a fixed chemical correction. The model should not directly prescribe an unrestricted chemical dose; instead, it should recommend a dose range within plantapproved upper and lower limits and verify the recommendation with conductivity, DOC/UV254 and ion data. This design allows AI to support chemical optimization while avoiding excessive sludge production, residual coagulant carryover and membrane-surface damage [\[121\]](#page-18-0).

In RO systems, model outputs should be translated into scaling and biofouling management actions [\[122\]](#page-18-0). A predicted increase in localized scaling risk may support pH correction, antiscalant-dose adjustment within product and manufacturer limits, recovery reduction or brinechannel flushing, but the action should be checked against saturation indices and online pH data [\[123\].](#page-18-0) A predicted biofouling risk may support targeted cleaning, localized biocide dosing, flow redistribution or inspection of modules with abnormal pressure-drop development, preferably confirmed by pressure-drop trends, ATP/EPS indicators or OCT/hyperspectral imaging when available [\[124\].](#page-18-0) Membrane autopsy or replacement should be considered only when normalized permeability, salt rejection or differential pressure cannot be restored after repeated cleaning and when multi-cycle evidence indicates persistent irreversible deterioration. Therefore, the practical role of AI is not to replace operator judgement, but to provide earlier, more specific and more defensible control recommendations [\[126\]](#page-18-0).

Across UF, NF and RO systems, the operational value of ML outputs depends on whether they can be converted into specific, bounded and verifiable control actions [\[127\].](#page-18-0) A practical control layer should therefore distinguish the model signal, the engineering interpretation, the possible intervention and the required safeguard before actuation [\[128\]](#page-18-0). This logic avoids the direct use of an unconstrained prediction for automatic control and emphasizes that every AI-assisted action should remain subject to plant limits, manufacturer recommendations, multisensor confirmation and operator approval.

## *4.3. UF: Sequence-aware GRU for hydraulic control*

## *4.3.1. Architectural advantages of GRU*

For UF systems, performance degradation is mainly driven by physical pore blockage and dynamic cake-layer compaction. These processes are rapid and cumulative [\[129\]](#page-18-0). Therefore, standard singlelayer ML algorithms, such as Support Vector Machines (SVM) or Random Forests (RF) are structurally inadequate [\[130\]](#page-18-0). These static models usually assume that observations at discrete time steps are statistically independent [\[131\].](#page-18-0) As a result, they ignore the historical and cumulative stress vectors that drive fouling evolution.

The GRU is a streamlined variant of Recurrent Neural Network (RNN). It is also computationally efficient. This architecture can effectively overcome the above limitation [\[132\]](#page-18-0). The structural advantage of the GRU framework comes from its internal mathematical gating mechanisms. These mechanisms include the Update Gate and the Reset Gate [\[133\].](#page-18-0) The update gate regulates information flow in a dynamic manner. It evaluates how much historical memory should be retained. This allows the network to capture continuous temporal dependencies over extended periods [\[134\]](#page-18-0). In contrast, the reset gate enables the model to discard redundant historical noise.

From a computational mathematics perspective, this streamlined gating mechanism effectively mitigates the vanishing gradient problem [\[135\].](#page-18-0) This problem is common in traditional RNN when they process long sequences [\[136\]](#page-18-0). More importantly, the GRU shows a clear computational advantage over the traditional Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) network [\[137\].](#page-18-0) This advantage becomes especially important in full-scale municipal water facilities with high-frequency SCADA data sampling. This superiority is rooted in the GRU architecture. Unlike LSTM, GRU deliberately omits the output gate. As a result, it significantly reduces the total number of trainable tensor parameters [\[138\]](#page-18-0). In high-frequency hydraulic control, this simplified architecture offers several benefits. It allows the GRU to maintain high temporal predictive accuracy [\[139\]](#page-18-0). It also accelerates backpropagation training. In addition, it reduces the memory footprint during inference [\[140\]](#page-18-0). This mathematical simplicity is the key reason why GRU is suitable for deployment on constrained local Industrial PCs (IPCs). It can also meet

the low latency requirements of real-time, variable-frequency backwash control [\[141\]](#page-18-0).

#### *4.3.2. Alignment with UF fouling and control actions*

As shown in [Fig. 2](#page-5-0), the intrinsic design of GRU aligns well with the high-frequency and dynamic characteristics of UF fouling. Municipal UF plants may experience sudden perturbations. These include seasonal algal blooms and massive turbidity shocks caused by extreme stormwater runoff. Under these conditions, the reset gate of GRU can rapidly cut off the baseline memory formed during previous stable periods [\[142\].](#page-18-0) This feature gives the model an immediate and highly sensitive adaptive capacity. It enables the model to respond effectively to extreme influent fluctuations.

By continuously processing time-variant and heterogeneous operational datasets, the GRU network can capture complex UF fouling dynamics. These datasets include influent flow, localized pressure, and specific colloidal loads. Through these inputs, the model can autonomously map the relationship between complex influent matrices and subsequent non-linear TMP escalation [\[143\].](#page-18-0) As a result, the GRU can predict the temporal thresholds that trigger sudden resistance spikes. This prediction can be achieved with high precision [\[144\].](#page-18-0) This predictive capability provides critical feed-forward input for real-time hydraulic control. It allows smart systems to adaptively adjust backwash frequency and permeate flux [\[145\]](#page-18-0). These adjustments can be made before the system reaches critical TMP thresholds. In this way, membrane stress can be reduced. Irreversible physical compaction of the cake layer can also be prevented.

From an environmental sustainability perspective, this sequenceaware architecture changes the operational logic of UF systems. It shifts UF operation from chemically intensive reactive cleaning to physically optimized preventive control [\[147\]](#page-18-0). By intercepting irreversible cake-layer compaction in advance, the GRU model can greatly reduce the frequency of mandatory chemical CIP interventions [\[148\]](#page-18-0). This strategic shift directly reduces the discharge of highly alkaline, acidic, and chlorine-rich secondary effluents into the receiving environment [\[149\]](#page-18-0). Furthermore, it lowers the carbon footprint associated with chemical production. It also reduces the excessive pumping energy that is usually wasted during severe fouling events [\[150\].](#page-18-0) Therefore, this algorithmic advancement goes beyond computational optimization. It is closely aligned with the environmental goals of sustainable and lowimpact water reclamation [\[151\]](#page-18-0).

## *4.4. NF: Regularized XGBoost for water-quality decoupling*

# *4.4.1. Architectural logic of XGBoost*

In NF systems, membrane performance is controlled by several interacting mechanisms. These include steric exclusion, hydrophobic affinities, and Donnan electrostatic shielding [\[152\].](#page-18-0) Datasets generated by full-scale NF facilities are often highly complex [\[153\].](#page-18-0) They usually show extreme high dimensionality, strong inter-variable coupling, and significant sensor background noise. A typical example is the severe multicollinearity among DOC, UV254, pH, and divalent cation concentrations [\[154\]](#page-18-0).

To navigate these complex matrices, the XGBoost model has become a preferred computational engine. XGBoost integrates multiple weak learners, mainly Classification and Regression Trees (CART), through an additive and incremental training framework [\[155\]](#page-18-0). In this framework, each newly added decision tree is mathematically designed to correct the prediction residuals left by previous models along the gradient direction [\[156\]](#page-18-0). The most prominent mathematical advantage of XGBoost lies in its highly rigorous regularized objective function. This function incorporates both L1 (Lasso) and L2 (Ridge) regularization penalty terms into the loss function [\[157\]](#page-18-0). These penalties constrain leaf node weights and overall tree depth. As a result, XGBoost effectively limits model complexity and strongly suppresses the overfitting risks commonly observed in deep learning models [\[158\].](#page-18-0) Furthermore,

XGBoost uses a second-order Taylor expansion to approximate the loss function. This allows the model to use both first-order gradients and second-order Hessians during training. Through this mechanism, it can identify optimal and sparsity-aware feature splitting points [\[159\].](#page-18-0)

#### *4.4.2. Alignment with NF fouling and dosing control*

The dual-regularization design of XGBoost is critical for decoupling the deeply synergistic mechanisms involved in NF fouling. As illustrated in [Fig. 2,](#page-5-0) XGBoost can isolate the specific mechanism by which multivalent metal cations, such as *Ca*<sup>2</sup><sup>+</sup> and *Mg*<sup>2</sup><sup>+</sup>, cross-link with certain NOM fractions. This interaction can intensify gel layer scaling. Importantly, XGBoost can identify this mechanism without being misled by standard operational fluctuations or overlapping DOM fluorescence signatures [\[160\].](#page-18-0) By combining this ensemble architecture with feature importance analysis, researchers can further map complex non-linear relationships. For example, they can clarify how sustained lowmolecular-weight organic loading contributes to later MWCO attenuation [\[161\]](#page-18-0). This analytical capability provides strong support for multiobjective optimization in NF processes. In traditional coagulation-NF hybrid systems, XGBoost can be used to continuously calculate the required chemical coagulant dosage according to dynamic raw water characteristics [\[162\].](#page-18-0) This targeted intervention improves the removal of problematic organic fractions in upstream treatment. It also helps preserve the reversibility of the NF fouling layer [\[163\].](#page-19-0) At the same time, it avoids secondary structural damage caused by residual chemicals, such as anionic polyacrylamide (APAM), accumulating on the membrane surface [\[164\].](#page-19-0)

The integration of regularized XGBoost can provide environmental benefits when it is linked to dosing control. By improving dynamic pretreatment dosing, the model may reduce over-dosing of aluminum or iron salts and polymeric flocculants [\[165\]](#page-19-0). More precise dosing can also reduce heavy-metal-laden sludge. Such sludge is difficult to dispose of safely [\[166\].](#page-19-0) In this sense, algorithmic decoupling of complex water matrices can support green-chemistry principles. It may reduce the chemical footprint of advanced back-end municipal water treatment [\[167\].](#page-19-0)

## *4.5. RO: CNN-GRU-SHAP for spatiotemporal diagnostics*

## *4.5.1. Need for multimodal spatiotemporal fusion*

RO systems are difficult predictive environments. The main reason is that biofouling and inorganic scaling are often localized and hidden [\[168\].](#page-19-0) Biofilm formation usually begins as a microscopic outbreak. It can then spread under favorable hydraulic and nutrient conditions [\[169\].](#page-19-0) A one-dimensional time-series model, such as a standalone GRU, may therefore miss spatial information. A tabular model, such as XGBoost, may also be insufficient when image or surface data are available [\[170\].](#page-19-0) These models do not directly describe the morphology of localized fouling layers. Hybrid CNN-GRU-SHAP architectures address this gap. They combine spatial feature extraction, temporal modeling, and interpretability in a single workflow [\[171\]](#page-19-0).

#### *4.5.2. Alignment with RO fouling and targeted cleaning*

Within this highly complex multimodal framework, as shown on the right side of [Fig. 2,](#page-5-0) the CNN acts as a non-linear extractor of high-order spatial features [\[172\].](#page-19-0) It can be coupled with non-invasive optical monitoring techniques, such as OCT or hyperspectral imaging. Through this coupling, the CNN uses localized multi-layer convolutional kernels to autonomously capture the morphological heterogeneity of the interfacial fouling layer [\[173\].](#page-19-0) In this context, the CNN functions as a "digital microscope". It mathematically distinguishes microstructural variations from 3D biophysical morphological profiles. This allows the model to detect the early stages of bacterial cell attachment or gypsum nucleation. These early fouling events can be identified long before they affect macroscopic system hydraulics [\[174\]](#page-19-0).

Subsequently, the dimensionally reduced spatial topological feature

maps are fused with macroscopic hydraulic time-series data. These data may include feed pressure, cross-flow velocity, and localized pH. The fused information is then fed into the downstream GRU module. Using its internal temporal gating mechanisms, the GRU dynamically constructs a predictive evolutionary model [\[175\]](#page-19-0). This model describes how the microscopic biofilms detected by the CNN may be further compacted under specific hydraulic stresses [\[176\]](#page-19-0). This spatiotemporal coupling gives the hybrid model high predictive precision when the multimodal dataset is reliable. It can accurately predict the temporal threshold at which localized microscopic scaling develops into macroscopic hydraulic failure.

#### *4.5.3. Deployment bottlenecks: Dimensionality and hardware*

The integration of non-invasive optical monitoring with CNN-GRU-SHAP is promising. However, full-scale deployment still faces major challenges. The most important challenge is dimensionality [\[177\]](#page-19-0). Highresolution 2D image matrices generate large data volumes. When these images are fused with high-frequency 1D hydraulic series, computation increases quickly. Memory demand also rises [\[178\]](#page-19-0). This issue is important for water facilities with legacy control hardware. Many plants still rely on local Programmable Logic Controllers (PLC). These PLCs usually cannot support heavy tensor operations without additional edgecomputing hardware [\[179\].](#page-19-0)

To address the gap between complex multimodal fusion and limited hardware capacity, future engineering deployments must include advanced feature dimensionality reduction. They must also incorporate model compression protocols [\[180\]](#page-19-0). Before large optical datasets are fed into recurrent temporal layers, robust latent-space extraction techniques should be applied. For linear feature spaces, Principal Component Analysis (PCA) can be used. For non-linear feature spaces, Variational Autoencoders (VAEs) are more suitable. VAEs can decode and extract non-linear topological invariants [\[181\]](#page-19-0). By converting macroscopic image tensors into compressed low-dimensional latent representations, the computational burden on the downstream GRU can be greatly reduced [\[182\]](#page-19-0).

Furthermore, dimensionality reduction should be combined with aggressive post-training model compression during edge deployment. These compression techniques include structural network pruning and precision quantization. For example, neural weights can be converted from FP32 to INT8 precision [\[183\].](#page-19-0) By streamlining the hybrid CNN-GRU network, low-latency models with reduced computational demands can be deployed directly on local edge computing devices within treatment facilities [\[184\].](#page-19-0) This edge-based deployment avoids severe cloud-communication latency. It also strengthens industrial data security [\[185\].](#page-19-0) More importantly, this hardware-software co-design demonstrates the practical feasibility of the CNN-GRU-SHAP architecture. It shows that the model can move beyond an idealized laboratory concept. It can become a robust and edge-deployable cognitive engine for fullscale digital twin water plants [\[186\].](#page-19-0)

#### **5. Performance benchmarking and deployment readiness**

Translating theoretical computational architectures into highly regulated full-scale industrial operations requires rigorous benchmark evaluations [\[187\]](#page-19-0). These evaluations should examine statistical accuracy, generalization capability, and engineering robustness under severe system perturbations.

#### *5.1. Accuracy, robustness and contextualized performance benchmarking*

The reported predictive performance of ML models should be interpreted with caution. Very high values, such as *R*2 values above 0.98–0.99 or RMSE reductions approaching 90%, are useful indicators of model potential, but they are not sufficient evidence of universal reliability [\[188\]](#page-19-0). Their engineering meaning depends strongly on dataset size, membrane process, prediction target, operating scale, validation

strategy, prediction horizon, operating range and the baseline model used for comparison [\[189\]](#page-19-0). Therefore, this review treats these values as context-dependent literature reports rather than general performance guarantees.

For structured tabular datasets, XGBoost has shown strong performance in selected membrane-related prediction tasks, including SEC estimation, salinity prediction, permeability-loss assessment, rejectiondrift evaluation and chemical-dose support [\[190\]](#page-19-0). In such cases, *R*<sup>2</sup> values close to 0.97–0.98 can be meaningful when the model is evaluated against appropriate baselines, such as linear regression, support vector regression, random forest, artificial neural networks or processempirical models [\[191\].](#page-19-0) However, the same *R*2 value may be less informative if the test set is small, randomly split from highly autocorrelated plant data, or restricted to a narrow operating range.

For UF fouling prediction, GRU models may achieve high *R*2 values when high-frequency TMP, flux, pressure, turbidity and backwashhistory data are available. This performance is most relevant when chronological splitting, rolling-origin validation, cross-season testing or independent module testing is used. Random train splitting can overestimate model reliability because adjacent SCADA observations are temporally correlated [\[192\].](#page-19-0) Therefore, GRU-based results should be reported together with sampling frequency, sequence length, prediction horizon, number of filtration cycles, operating days and shock-load events used for training and testing [\[193\]](#page-19-0).

For RO or multimodal fouling diagnosis, CNN-GRU and CNN-GRU-SHAP models can substantially reduce RMSE when image, OCT, hyperspectral or spatially resolved sensor data are fused with hydraulic time series [\[194\].](#page-19-0) However, large RMSE reductions should be interpreted relative to the baseline model and the absolute error scale. A 90% RMSE reduction compared with a weak standalone baseline does not necessarily imply equivalent robustness under full-scale plant operation [\[195\].](#page-19-0) Such results should be evaluated using independent modules, different feedwater regimes, external test conditions or longer-term validation whenever possible.

To avoid overstatement, high-performance metrics should be reported together with explicit contextual information rather than being isolated in a descriptive comparison table [\[196\]](#page-19-0). If the original study does not report a specific item, this limitation should be stated directly rather than inferred. At a minimum, each ML-based membrane-fouling study should identify the membrane process, dominant prediction target, dataset scale, operating range, validation strategy, prediction horizon, baseline model and direct process-control relevance [\[15\]](#page-15-0). This reporting logic allows readers to distinguish between model accuracy obtained under narrow experimental conditions and model robustness that is likely to be transferable to plant-scale membrane operation [\[197\].](#page-19-0)

For UF studies using GRU or other recurrent models, the reported inputs should include high-frequency TMP, flux, pressure, permeate flow, turbidity, particle count, temperature, filtration time, backwash history, air-scouring intensity and cleaning records where available. The corresponding outputs should be stated as measurable operating endpoints, such as TMP increase, normalized flux decline, permeability loss, threshold-crossing probability or time to the next backwash/CIP event [\[198\].](#page-19-0) These studies should also report sampling frequency, sequence length, number of filtration cycles, operating days, module scale, shockload events and missing-data rate, and they should be validated by chronological splitting, rolling-origin testing, cross-season testing or independent module testing against baselines such as persistence models, ARIMA, SVR, random forest, LSTM or process-empirical control rules [\[199\]](#page-19-0).

For NF studies using regularized XGBoost, the minimum contextual information should include DOC, UV254, fluorescence indices, pH, conductivity, ionic strength, *Ca*<sup>2</sup><sup>+</sup>, *SO* <sup>2</sup><sup>−</sup> <sup>4</sup> , alkalinity, temperature, pressure, recovery, flux, coagulant dose and pretreatment conditions. Outputs should be linked to permeability decline, rejection drift, MWCO attenuation risk, scaling or gel-layer risk, SEC, salinity or recommended chemical-dose ranges. The dataset description should include sample number, water-quality variables, operating days, membrane type, feed matrix, laboratory/pilot/full-scale status and the measured ranges of pH, conductivity, flux and recovery [\[200\]](#page-19-0). Validation should use k-fold or nested cross-validation for independent tabular datasets, or chronological and rolling validation for plant data, with comparisons against linear regression, SVR, random forest, ANN and empirical dosing rules [\[201\].](#page-19-0)

For RO or multimodal studies using CNN-GRU, CNN-GRU-SHAP or related architectures, the input space should be described through OCT images, hyperspectral images, surface or module images, pressure, differential pressure, flow, recovery, pH, conductivity, temperature, antiscalant dose, normalized salt passage and cleaning history. The prediction target should be expressed as localized biofouling or scaling risk, surface-fouling evolution, TMP or pressure-drop escalation, time to hydraulic deterioration or a module-specific warning score [\[202\]](#page-19-0). These studies should report the number of images or scans, spatial resolution, imaging modality, synchronized SCADA records, module or coupon scale, operating duration and image-preprocessing method. Independent module testing, time-separated validation, external feedwater testing or cross-site testing is preferable, and comparisons should include standalone CNN, standalone GRU, RF/XGBoost and rule-based alarm baselines [\[203\].](#page-19-0) When SHAP or another XAI method is coupled with UF, NF or RO prediction models, the same input variables, timestamped records and quality-control flags used by the predictive model should be retained in the explanation layer [\[204\]](#page-19-0). The resulting driver attribution should be checked against operating records, operator knowledge, cleaning logs and known membrane chemistry before it is translated into a dose, cleaning or inspection recommendation.

Therefore, the comparison of GRU, XGBoost, CNN-GRU-SHAP and XAI-assisted models should not be interpreted as a ranking based only on *R*2 or RMSE. It should instead be understood as a process-specific assessment of data availability, validation rigor, control relevance and deployment risk [\[205\]](#page-19-0). This interpretation is more consistent with fullscale membrane operation, where the practical value of an ML model depends not only on prediction accuracy but also on robustness, interpretability, operator trust and compatibility with existing SCADA and control infrastructure [\[206\].](#page-19-0)

# *5.2. SCADA noise, data sparsity and fallback control*

Reliable deployment requires explicit consideration of engineering data quality. Operational datasets from full-scale facilities are fundamentally different from datasets collected under controlled laboratory conditions [\[207\]](#page-19-0). In industrial SCADA systems, sensor drift, background noise, maintenance interruptions and communication failures are common [\[208\].](#page-19-0)

In such harsh data environments, the XGBoost framework shows exceptional engineering robustness. Its built-in sparsity-aware splitfinding algorithm can automatically learn optimal default paths for missing data [\[209\]](#page-20-0). This process occurs during the construction of decision trees. Therefore, XGBoost can reliably process SCADA logs that contain missing values and sensor disconnections. It does not need to rely heavily on complex frontend data imputation protocols [\[210\]](#page-20-0). This is important because such protocols may introduce bias.

In sharp contrast, sequence-aware deep learning models are highly sensitive to data integrity. This applies to both single-layer GRUs and complex CNN-GRU architectures. Missing values can directly break the temporal continuity required by recurrent gating mechanisms [\[211\].](#page-20-0) As a result, the network may lose its ability to retain or update historical states. To overcome this structural vulnerability, a robust frontend data imputation protocol is essential. However, current advanced imputation algorithms involve clear computational trade-offs [\[212\].](#page-20-0) These tradeoffs are especially important when distinguishing between Missing

Completely At Random (MCAR) and Missing Not At Random (MNAR) scenarios [\[213\].](#page-20-0)

Before using computationally heavy reconstruction models, watertreatment plants should first evaluate simple estimation tools that are already familiar to process engineers [\[214\]](#page-20-0). Two practical options are moving-window filtering and Kalman-type state estimation. A moving median or exponentially weighted moving average (EWMA) can remove isolated spikes in TMP, flow, conductivity, pH or turbidity signals caused by electrical noise, short probe fouling or communication jitter [\[215\]](#page-20-0). It is most appropriate when the missing interval is short, the process is near steady state and the objective is alarm stabilization rather than reconstruction of a true hydraulic shock [\[216\].](#page-20-0) The window should be shorter than the intended early-warning horizon; otherwise, real rapid fouling events may be smoothed out. Kalman filtering is more suitable when a plant has a simple dynamic model for a slowly varying state, such as normalized permeability, TMP trend or permeate conductivity [\[217\].](#page-20-0) It combines the model-predicted state with noisy sensor observations and can provide online estimates with uncertainty. Therefore, it is useful for RO/NF monitoring under gradually changing recovery, temperature and pressure conditions [\[218\].](#page-20-0) However, Kalman-type methods should not be used blindly during abrupt influent shocks unless the process model and noise assumptions are recalibrated [\[219\].](#page-20-0)

For transient MCAR scenarios, KNN interpolation offers fast execution. However, it often fails to capture abrupt and non-stationary hydraulic shock loads. Spline smoothing also has limitations. It may excessively smooth temporal sequences. This can mask critical earlywarning signals of sudden physical pore blockage. For highly complex MNAR scenarios, advanced deep learning-based reconstruction methods can provide high-fidelity results [\[220\].](#page-20-0) Denoising Autoencoders (DAE) are one representative example. Recent progress in environmental informatics has also introduced new approaches. These include Graph Neural Networks (GNN) and Transformer-based spatiotemporal attention mechanisms. GNNs can use spatial topological relationships among distributed SCADA sensors [\[221\]](#page-20-0). Transformers can capture long-range temporal dependencies without relying on rigid sequential constraints [\[222\].](#page-20-0) Together, these methods expand the theoretical ability to handle multivariable and non-stationary hydraulic missing sequences. Nevertheless, these advanced architectures still face practical barriers. Although they perform well in theoretical MNAR reconstruction, they usually have very large computational footprints [\[223\].](#page-20-0) This makes lowlatency edge deployment difficult. They also carry a serious risk of error propagation. For example, an imputation algorithm may hallucinate an abnormal hydraulic profile during a prolonged sensor blackout [\[224\]](#page-20-0). These synthetic errors may then be amplified by the downstream GRU update gates. In the worst case, this could trigger harmful false-positive CIP interventions.

A reliable predictive framework should not depend on a single algorithm. It should include a physics-informed dynamic fallback mechanism. The mechanism can use data-integrity thresholds as operational safeguards. Short signal losses may be handled by imputation when the missing rate is low and the sensor pattern remains physically plausible [\[225\].](#page-20-0) Severe or sustained communication failures require a different strategy. In such cases, the system should downgrade to a simpler and more robust control layer [\[226\]](#page-20-0). The exact thresholds should be calibrated for each plant, sensor network, and safety requirement.

Under data-sparse conditions, the framework can suspend GRU and CNN-GRU modules. This avoids unstable recurrent updates [\[227\]](#page-20-0). Control authority can then return to a regularized XGBoost ensemble or to conservative rule-based control [\[228\]](#page-20-0). XGBoost is useful because it can handle missing branches during split finding. This reduces dependence on aggressive imputation. The fallback mechanism protects the operational baseline until sensor integrity is restored [\[229\].](#page-20-0)

## **6. Explainable and safe control strategies**

In highly regulated engineering sectors, operators and process

engineers are unlikely to surrender control of critical membrane assets to black-box algorithms [\[230\]](#page-20-0). This is especially true in municipal drinking water safety assurance and large-scale industrial zero-liquid discharge (ZLD) systems. These algorithms must provide transparent physical and logical explanations [\[231\].](#page-20-0) Therefore, accurately forecasting a transmembrane pressure (TMP) surge is only a partial solution. The ultimate survival and deployment of a predictive model in full-scale industrial operations depend heavily on robust interpretability. This means that the model must translate complex mathematical vectors into clear and actionable physical or chemical intervention protocols [\[233\]](#page-20-0).

#### *6.1. Native feature importance versus SHAP*

Compared with deep neural networks, tree-based ensemble models such as XGBoost have inherent advantages in interpretability [\[234\]](#page-20-0). During the construction of decision trees, XGBoost automatically calculates global native feature importance scores. These scores are usually based on information gain or feature frequency [\[235\].](#page-20-0) Such scores provide water treatment plants with macroscopic "priority lists" of fouling contributors. For example, they can identify the hydrophobic fraction of DOM as the main cause of permeability decline [\[236\].](#page-20-0) This interpretation may be more informative than simply attributing the decline to bulk influent turbidity [\[237\]](#page-20-0).

However, native feature importance in tree-based models only provides globally averaged correlations. It cannot perform "instance-level" micro-tracing for localized or sudden fouling events at a specific operational moment [\[238\]](#page-20-0). This limitation highlights the necessity of incorporating SHAP analysis [\[239\]](#page-20-0). SHAP is rooted in cooperative game theory. It goes beyond basic correlation analysis. Specifically, it calculates the exact marginal contribution of each physicochemical input to a specific prediction [\[240\]](#page-20-0). These inputs may include precise temperature fluctuations, localized pH variations, or transient spikes in molecular weight distributions. SHAP then explains how each factor contributes to the deviation of a prediction from its baseline expectation [\[241\]](#page-20-0). Whether the predictive core is XGBoost or a hybrid CNN-GRU model, SHAP can serve as a high-resolution diagnostic tool. It effectively reduces the explanatory barriers associated with deep learning models [\[242\].](#page-20-0)

## *6.2. Operator-facing XAI: From predictions to physical interventions*

For membrane operators, the practical value of XAI is not the mathematical decomposition itself. Its value lies in converting a warning into an auditable reason for action [\[243\]](#page-20-0). In an XAI-enabled membrane dashboard, each prediction should be accompanied by four elements: the predicted risk or lead time, the dominant positive drivers, the physically plausible fouling mechanism, and the recommended bounded response [\[244\]](#page-20-0). SHAP is particularly useful because it explains why a specific alarm deviates from the normal baseline, rather than only reporting globally important variables [\[245\]](#page-20-0).

At the plant scale, SHAP or related local attribution methods can support five operator-facing tasks. First, they can identify dominant fouling drivers by ranking variables such as TMP slope, differential pressure, normalized permeability, UV254/DOC, *Ca*<sup>2</sup><sup>+</sup>/*Mg*<sup>2</sup><sup>+</sup>, sulfate saturation, pH deviation, recovery, antiscalant dose, temperature, ATP/ EPS indicators, or OCT-derived biofilm thickness [\[246\]](#page-20-0). Second, they can distinguish likely fouling modes. A risk dominated by turbidity and TMP slope suggests cake-layer compaction, whereas a risk dominated by pH, calcium, sulfate, recovery, and antiscalant variables suggests scaling [\[13\]](#page-15-0). Third, they provide a defensible basis for cleaning decisions. A hydraulic signature may justify shorter filtration cycles or backwashing, whereas persistent chemical or biological attributions may justify chemically enhanced backwash, localized biocide dosing, or CIP [\[247\]](#page-20-0). Fourth, they can support chemical dosing by indicating whether risk is driven mainly by coagulant-sensitive DOM, pH-sensitive scaling, or under-dosed antiscalant. Fifth, they can warn operators about abnormal

conditions [\[249\]](#page-20-0). If the model error is high or the SHAP pattern is inconsistent with known fouling chemistry, the system should flag possible sensor drift, data loss, unusual influent quality, or actuator malfunction instead of issuing an automatic command [\[250\]](#page-20-0).

For example, a hybrid RO model may predict that TMP or differential pressure will exceed an alarm threshold within the next 48 h. A conventional black-box alarm would only tell the operator that fouling risk is high. A SHAP-enhanced alarm can indicate whether the main contribution comes from localized biofilm thickness, rising pressure drop, pH drift away from the antiscalant chelation range, increased recovery, or elevated scaling tendency [\[251\].](#page-20-0) The recommended response can therefore be more selective: pH correction and antiscalant adjustment for scaling-driven risk; localized biocide dosing and module inspection for biofouling-driven risk; brine flushing or recovery reduction for supersaturation-driven risk; and full-line CIP only when repeated warnings and poor post-cleaning recovery indicate irreversible deterioration [\[252\].](#page-20-0)

The architecture in [Fig. 4](#page-12-0) should therefore be interpreted as an operator-supervised diagnostic loop rather than a fully unconstrained autonomous controller. The CNN-GRU module provides an early warning, SHAP converts the warning into ranked physical or chemical drivers, and the decision-support layer maps these drivers to bounded interventions. Post-intervention data, such as TMP recovery, normalized flux, salt passage, chemical consumption, and module pressure drop, are then returned to the model for feedback calibration.

Importantly, XAI outputs should be treated as decision support rather than proof of causality [\[253\].](#page-20-0) A high SHAP value for pH, *Ca*<sup>2</sup><sup>+</sup>, or OCT-derived biofilm thickness should be checked against plant observations, water chemistry, cleaning history, and manufacturer limits before it is converted into dosing or cleaning action [\[254\]](#page-20-0). This humanin-the-loop design allows XAI to improve operator trust while avoiding unsafe over-reaction to a single model prediction [\[255\].](#page-20-0)

In practical use, XAI outputs should be converted into bounded operating responses according to the dominant physical or chemical driver [\[256\].](#page-20-0) When local attribution is dominated by TMP slope, turbidity or flux decline, the warning is most consistent with rapid cakelayer formation or compaction; the corresponding response is to shorten the filtration cycle, increase backwashing or reduce flux while confirming that TMP/flux limits and hydraulic recovery remain acceptable [\[257\].](#page-20-0) When UV254, DOC, divalent-cation indicators or pH dominate the explanation, the likely concern is organic-inorganic fouling or charge shielding, so coagulant dose, pH and pretreatment settings should be adjusted only within approved chemical-dose ranges [\[258\]](#page-21-0).

For RO operation, attributions dominated by pH, recovery, sulfate saturation or antiscalant dose point to localized scaling, for which pH correction, antiscalant adjustment, recovery reduction or brine-channel flushing should be checked against saturation indices and membrane limits [\[259\].](#page-21-0) By contrast, OCT-derived biofilm thickness, ATP/EPS indicators or pressure-drop increase indicate localized biofouling risk and should trigger module inspection, targeted cleaning or localized biocide dosing after confirmation by pressure-drop trends or microbial indicators [\[260\].](#page-21-0) High residual error or inconsistent attribution patterns should be treated as possible sensor drift, data loss or abnormal influent disturbance; alarm confirmation, conservative fallback control and human review should therefore precede automatic actuation [\[261\]](#page-21-0).

## *6.3. Critical barriers to trustworthy ML-based membrane control*

Several barriers still limit the direct translation of ML predictions into autonomous membrane control. First, many published studies rely on random train-test splits from a single experimental campaign or one treatment facility [\[262\].](#page-21-0) This practice can overestimate predictive performance because adjacent time points often share the same hydraulic history, influent regime and cleaning cycle. Future studies should report chronological validation, cross-season testing and, where possible, cross-site validation.

Second, high statistical accuracy does not automatically imply safe control [\[263\].](#page-21-0) A model that predicts TMP accurately may still recommend an unsafe operating action if the control boundary is not constrained by membrane integrity, allowable recovery, cleaning chemistry, regulatory limits or operator practice. Therefore, ML outputs should be embedded in supervisory control layers that include hard safety limits, alarm confirmation and human override [\[264\]](#page-21-0).

Third, explainability methods should be interpreted carefully. SHAP values can identify variables that contribute strongly to a model prediction, but they do not prove mechanistic causality by themselves [\[265\].](#page-21-0) In membrane systems, attribution results should be checked against known fouling chemistry, mass-transfer limits and plant observations before they are converted into chemical dosing or cleaning decisions [\[266\].](#page-21-0)

Finally, open and standardized datasets remain scarce in the

membrane sector [\[267\]](#page-21-0). This limits fair comparison among GRU, XGBoost, CNN-GRU and emerging Transformer or graph-based models. Future benchmarking should report sensor lists, sampling frequency, missing-data rate, cleaning events, validation design, uncertainty metrics and deployment constraints [\[268\].](#page-21-0) These details are essential for assessing whether a model is only accurate in one dataset or transferable to real water-process operation [\[269\]](#page-21-0).

#### **8. Future perspectives**

Future research should move beyond laboratory-scale model demonstrations toward validated, safe and transferable control systems for full-scale membrane facilities. Four directions are particularly important: physics-informed learning, plant-wide digital twins, privacypreserving model training and open benchmarking with standardized fouling metrics and techno-economic assessment.

As illustrated in Fig. 5, the future development of smart membranebased water treatment can be understood as a staged evolution from data-driven monitoring to physics-informed autonomous operation. In the first phase, physical water treatment plants are equipped with sensors, instrumentation, actuators and chemical dosing units. These devices generate real-time hydraulic, water-quality and operational data. In the second phase, these data streams are processed by edgecomputing nodes and physics-informed neural networks. The models no longer depend only on statistical correlations. Instead, they are constrained by physical laws, such as fluid dynamics, mass transfer and membrane transport equations. In the third phase, the physical plant is connected to a virtual water treatment plant. This digital-twin environment allows cleaning protocols, dosing strategies and hydraulic adjustments to be tested in a zero-physical-risk simulation space before they are applied to the real system. Therefore, Fig. 5 extends the central logic of this review. It shows how mechanism-aligned ML can evolve from fouling prediction to safe process control and finally to autonomous optimization in next-generation water treatment facilities.

As shown in Fig. 5, the roadmap shows the transition from datadriven physical plants to physics-informed edge computing and autonomy supported by digital twins. Sensor data, edge-computed information and control commands are integrated with PINN-based physical constraints. This integration supports virtual testing of cleaning, dosing and backwash strategies before plant-level implementation.

#### *8.1. Thermodynamic robustness through PINNs*

Future ML frameworks should move beyond purely data-driven correlations. Physics-Informed Machine Learning (PIML) provides one important pathway. Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) can embed governing equations into the loss function. These equations may include Navier–Stokes flow, concentration polarization, Donnan steric transport or membrane mass-transfer terms. This design can constrain model predictions with mass conservation, energy conservation and transport physics. It may reduce the risk of physically invalid control recommendations under hydraulic shocks, influent disturbances or SCADA noise.

For membrane-fouling prediction, three governing equations are particularly suitable for PINN embedding, but they should be written in explicit vector form so that the residual terms remain physically meaningful.

$$\nabla u = 0 \text{ and } \rho \left( \frac{\partial u}{\partial t} + u \nabla u \right) = -\nabla p + \mu \nabla^2 u$$
 (1)

This equation represents incompressible continuity and momentum conservation in the feed channel. In a PINN, its residual can constrain cross-flow velocity, pressure drop and shear stress, and it is most useful when local hydrodynamics controls particle deposition, cake compression or biofilm detachment. Here, *μ* is the velocity vector, *ρ* is fluid density, *t* is time, *p* is pressure, *μ* is dynamic viscosity, ∇ is the gradient/

divergence operator and ∇2 is the Laplacian operator.

$$\frac{\partial C}{\partial t} + u\nabla C = \nabla(D\nabla C) - R(C) \tag{2}$$

This equation represents advection-diffusion-reaction mass balance. It can constrain concentration polarization, solute accumulation and scaling-precursor transport near the membrane surface, especially in NF and RO systems where *Ca*<sup>2</sup><sup>+</sup>, *SO* <sup>2</sup><sup>−</sup> <sup>4</sup> , DOM or nutrients accumulate at the interface. Here, *C* is solute or foulant concentration, *D* is the effective diffusion coefficient, and *R*(*C*) is the net reaction, precipitation, biological-growth or removal term. When *D* is constant, ∇(*D*∇*C*) can be simplified to *D*∇<sup>2</sup>*C*.

$$Jv = \frac{\Delta P - \Delta \pi}{\left[\mu(Rm + Rf)\right] \text{ and } Rf}, Rf = Rc + Rp + Rb$$
(3)

This equation represents Darcy's law combined with a resistance-inseries expression and an osmotic-pressure correction. It can constrain the relationship among water flux, TMP, osmotic pressure, viscosity and fouling resistance, making it suitable for predicting TMP rise, permeability loss and cleaning thresholds in UF, NF and RO operation. Here, *Jv*  is permeate flux, Δ*p* is the applied transmembrane pressure, Δ*π* is the osmotic pressure difference across the membrane, *Rm* is intrinsic membrane resistance, *Rf* is total fouling resistance, and *Rc*, *Rp* and *Rb*  denote cake/gel-layer resistance, pore-blocking or pore-constriction resistance and biofilm-related resistance, respectively. For UF, Δ*π*is often small and may be neglected, whereas for NF and RO it should be retained to avoid thermodynamically unrealistic flux predictions.

In practice, the PINN loss function can combine the data-fitting error with weighted residual penalties from one or more of these equations. The selected equation should match the available sensors and the target control action, rather than being inserted only to increase model complexity.

This direction is particularly important for membrane operation because fouling is not only a statistical pattern. It is also governed by physical deposition, chemical interaction, biological growth and hydraulic stress. For UF systems, PINNs may help constrain GRU-based TMP prediction within realistic hydraulic boundaries. For NF systems, physics-informed constraints may improve the interpretation of organic–inorganic coupling and Donnan shielding. For RO systems, PINNs may support safer prediction of localized scaling and biofilm growth under changing recovery, pH and antiscalant conditions. In this way, physics-informed learning can strengthen the mechanistic reliability of the ML architectures discussed in this review.

## *8.2. Plant-wide digital twins for multi-barrier optimization*

Current predictive modeling often focuses on individual membrane modules. Future work should scale these models toward plant-wide digital twins. This is important for multi-barrier treatment trains, such as *O*3-coagulation-UF-NF systems, RO-based wastewater reuse lines and decentralized drinking-water networks. In these systems, fouling risk does not develop in a single unit alone. Upstream coagulation, oxidation, filtration and chemical dosing can strongly influence downstream membrane resistance.

As shown in [Fig. 5](#page-13-0), a digital twin can connect the physical water treatment plant with a virtual plant. The virtual plant can simulate hydraulic conditions, cleaning protocols, chemical dosing and backwash optimization before real-world implementation. This provides a safer control environment than direct trial-and-error operation. Edge computing can help synchronize virtual simulations with physical plant data. Multimodal models can then serve as cognitive engines for early warning, process diagnosis and control optimization. The goal is to simulate cross-stage fouling propagation before failure occurs. Such plant-wide digital twins could help operators shift from reactive membrane cleaning to predictive and preventive process management.

## *8.3. Federated learning to overcome data silos*

Advanced deep neural networks can provide strong diagnostic precision. However, their generalization is still limited by data silos in the water sector. Many utilities cannot share high-resolution SCADA records because of infrastructure security, commercial confidentiality and datagovernance constraints. This limitation makes it difficult to train robust models across different water matrices, membrane materials, operating modes and climatic conditions.

Federated learning offers a possible solution. It allows models to be trained at local edge nodes while only protected model updates are exchanged among facilities. Raw operational data can remain within each plant. This approach may support more generalizable AI models while protecting sensitive plant information. In the context of [Fig. 5](#page-13-0), federated learning can extend the digital twin concept from a single plant to a distributed network of smart water treatment facilities. Each facility can contribute to shared model improvement without exposing its raw SCADA data. This strategy may be especially useful for validating GRU, XGBoost, CNN-GRU-SHAP and PINN-based models across different sites.

Despite these opportunities, federated learning should not be treated as a complete solution by itself. Differences in sensor configuration, sampling frequency, cleaning records, membrane type and influent composition can still cause model bias. Therefore, future studies should combine federated learning with standardized data dictionaries, uncertainty quantification, cross-site validation and operator-supervised control boundaries. Only through this combined strategy can MLbased membrane control become both transferable and trustworthy.

#### *8.4. Open benchmarks, standardized fouling metrics and technoeconomic assessment*

To make these future directions operational, the field needs shared benchmarks, standardized fouling indicators and techno-economic evaluation rules that are explicitly linked to plant operation. Open UF, NF and RO datasets should report the process type, feed matrix, membrane and module type, SCADA variables, water-quality variables, sampling frequency, missing-data rate, pretreatment conditions, chemical-dosing records, cleaning events, abnormal-operation labels and independent test periods. For time-series data, chronological, seasonal and cross-site test sets should be provided. For image-based RO or NF studies, OCT, hyperspectral or surface-imaging data should be synchronized with hydraulic and chemical variables. This structure would allow GRU, XGBoost, CNN-GRU-SHAP, PINN and future Transformer or graph-based models to be compared under the same engineering conditions.

Standardized fouling and control metrics should also be reported to connect model accuracy with operator-readable membrane status. Recommended indicators include normalized permeability, TMP slope, flux decline, pressure drop, salt passage, rejection drift, cleaning frequency, recovery after cleaning, chemical dose and SEC. Model validation should move beyond random data splitting and include chronological splitting, rolling-origin validation, seasonal testing, crossmodule testing, cross-site testing and uncertainty intervals. These requirements would reduce over-optimistic accuracy claims and test whether a model remains transferable under different influent matrices, membrane modules and operating histories.

Finally, future plant-scale studies should evaluate baseline operation against AI-assisted operation over sufficiently long periods and quantify energy use, chemical consumption, CIP frequency, membrane lifetime, water recovery, downtime, sludge production, greenhouse-gas implications and the risk of off-specification permeate. Techno-economic reporting should include sensor and edge-hardware CAPEX, energy, chemical, labor, membrane-replacement and downtime OPEX, payback period and sensitivity analysis. Before autonomous control is attempted, studies should also define control-readiness conditions, including safety

<span id="page-15-0"></span>limits, actuator constraints, alarm confirmation, human override, cybersecurity requirements and fallback-control logic. Together, these reporting elements would convert model outputs into bounded and auditable operating actions and make plant-scale validation, physicsinformed learning, digital twins and federated learning easier to judge by utilities, regulators and technology providers.

From an engineering implementation perspective, water utilities should first establish a reliable data foundation by auditing SCADA tags, calibrating key sensors, standardizing fouling indicators and linking operating data with cleaning and dosing logs. They should then deploy AI models in an advisory mode, where predictions are benchmarked against operator decisions and translated into bounded recommendations for flux, backwash, pH, antiscalant or coagulant adjustment under explicit safety constraints. After sufficient plant-scale validation, these advisory models can be connected to model predictive control and digital twins so that utilities can gradually shift from alarm-driven reactive cleaning to AI-assisted predictive control with human override and fallback protection.

## **9. Conclusions**

Membrane fouling remains a major barrier to stable, low-carbon and chemically efficient membrane operation. This review shows that model selection should be driven by fouling mechanism, data structure and control objective rather than by algorithmic complexity alone. UF systems require models that can follow rapid cake-layer development and hydraulic memory. GRU models are suitable when high-frequency timeseries data are reliable and when the output is linked to adaptive backwashing or flux control. NF systems require models that can handle coupled organic, inorganic and electrostatic variables. Regularized XGBoost is useful because it can decouple high-dimensional tabular features and tolerate missing values. It is especially valuable when the output supports pretreatment or dosing decisions. RO systems require models that can capture spatially localized biofouling and scaling. Hybrid CNN-GRU models are suitable when optical or image-based data are available and when the results are linked to targeted cleaning, pH correction or antiscalant control.

This review further emphasizes that high prediction accuracy alone is not sufficient for engineering deployment. Predictive models must be interpretable, robust to SCADA noise and compatible with plant-level safety constraints. XAI methods, especially SHAP, are needed to translate model outputs into operator-readable physical or chemical interventions. Fallback control is also necessary when data integrity is weakened by sensor drift, signal loss or communication failures. Overall, future ML-based fouling control should be evaluated by deployability rather than prediction accuracy alone. A useful framework should provide reproducible benchmarks, use standardized fouling indicators and demonstrate economic and environmental value under plant-relevant constraints.
