# A B S T R A C T

Efficient and reliable desalination through seawater reverse osmosis (SWRO) mandates optimized pre-treatment strategies to minimize organic and inorganic fouling. Coagulation, the process of agglomerating colloidal particles using chemical coagulants, in combination with media filtration to reduce colloidal fouling on reverse osmosis membranes is commonly used in seawater pretreatment. Due to its inherent complexity and the absence of physical models to quantify the efficiency of coagulation, overdosing of coagulants is ubiquitously observed to maintain filtered water quality. To address this problem, we use Artificial neural networks (ANNs) to optimize coagulant dosing by predicting the SDI after chemical dosing. The model is developed by using large-scale plant data comprising of different seawater physical parameters and plant operational data including pH, SDI, turbidity, coagulant dosing rate, and flocculant dosing rate. By using feature engineering, selection, and our domain knowledge, new input parameters are derived, irrelevant parameters are eliminated, and these are used as inputs to train the model. The developed ANNs model achieved a prediction accuracy of 95% also outperforms other machine learning methods, and upon industrialization it reduced annual coagulant consumption by 11.7% when implemented in a commercial SWRO plant producing 216,000 m<sup>3</sup>/day of desalinated water.

# **1. Introduction**

Freshwater scarcity, one of the biggest challenges of our time, continues to expand across different regions ([Elimelech & Phillip,](#page-7-0) [2011](#page-7-0); [Ghaffour, Missimer, & Amy,](#page-7-1) [2013b](#page-7-1)). This issue is escalated further by the continuous growth of population in inherently water stressed regions ([Elimelech & Phillip,](#page-7-0) [2011](#page-7-0); [Mekonnen & Hoekstra,](#page-7-2) [2016](#page-7-2)). To address this, seawater reverse osmosis (SWRO), the most efficient technology to generate freshwater from seawater ([Altmann et al.,](#page-7-3) [2022](#page-7-3); [Qasim, Badrelzaman, Darwish, Darwish, & Hilal,](#page-7-4) [2019\)](#page-7-4), is relied upon in many regions including the Middle East, Africa, the US west coast, and the Southern part of Europe ([Ghaffour, Missimer, & Amy](#page-7-5), [2013a](#page-7-5); [Gude, Nirmalakhandan, & Deng,](#page-7-6) [2011](#page-7-6)). The technology is based on the separation of water and inorganic salt solute from seawater through a semi-permeable membrane [\(Das, Arunachalam, Ahmad, Manalastas, &](#page-7-7) [Mishra,](#page-7-7) [2019](#page-7-7); [Gonzalez-Gil et al.,](#page-7-8) [2021](#page-7-8)). SWRO utilizes energy in form of potential energy to counter the osmotic gradient of seawater to produce low salinity permeate and concentrated brine. To further increase the efficiency and reliability of the SWRO process, its operation is complemented with rigorous pre-treatment technologies and processes from bar screen, media filtration, dissolved air flotation, disinfection, coagulation, de-chlorination, and acidification, among others [\(Altmann](#page-7-9) [& Das,](#page-7-9) [2021](#page-7-9); [Voutchkov](#page-8-0), [2010](#page-8-0), [2012\)](#page-8-1).

The SWRO process has undergone several enhancements over the last decade, it still remains an energy and chemical-intensive process and this intensifies environmental stress ([Elimelech & Phillip](#page-7-0), [2011;](#page-7-0) [Ghaffour et al.,](#page-7-1) [2013b](#page-7-1); [Hasanin et al.,](#page-7-10) [2023;](#page-7-10) [Karabelas, Kout](#page-7-11)[sou, Kostoglou, & Sioutopoulos,](#page-7-11) [2018;](#page-7-11) [Voutchkov](#page-8-2), [2018\)](#page-8-2). The pressing demand to squeeze energy consumption to its near theoretical level has prompted research primarily focusing on improving the energy efficiency of the process. The use of novel materials to develop next generation desalination membranes with ultra-high water permeability and high water–solute selectivity has been thoroughly explored ([Werber, Osuji, & Elimelech,](#page-8-3) [2016](#page-8-3)). Despite substantial research efforts towards the development of highly efficient membranes, the resulting reduction in energy consumption has been minimal; this calls for a paradigm shift in the current desalination research approach towards rational system design, as well as the optimization of process operations ([Patel et al.](#page-7-12), [2020;](#page-7-12) [Yousry, Ridwan, Altmann, Rousseva, Azab, & Das](#page-8-4), [2022\)](#page-8-4). While many physical models could be utilized to achieve this, however, the complexity of the processes engender inefficient use of computation usage and inaccurate results ([Dudchenko & Mauter](#page-7-13), [2020\)](#page-7-13).

The coagulation process is employed to remove suspended solids by destabilizing the particle using chemicals to agglomerate the particles ([Gonzalez-Gil](#page-7-8) [et al.](#page-7-8), [2021\)](#page-7-8). The coagulant demand of seawater depends on certain parameters such as its salinity, pH, presence of humic substances and algal organic matter ([Duan, Niu, Shi, Wilson, & Graham,](#page-7-14) [2009](#page-7-14); [Shutova et al.](#page-7-15), [2016](#page-7-15)). For an effective pretreatment, coagulant dosing needs optimization. Dosing lesser than what is required will result in ineffective removal of organic matter, resulting in biofouling on the membrane ([Tabatabai, Schippers, & Kennedy](#page-8-5), [2014](#page-8-5)), while dosing excess will result in inorganic fouling on the RO membrane which can lead to membrane oxidation damage ([Chesters et al.,](#page-7-16) [2013;](#page-7-16) [Gabelich,](#page-7-17) [Yun, & Coffey](#page-7-17), [2002](#page-7-17); [Gonzalez-Gil et al.](#page-7-8), [2021](#page-7-8)). The coagulant dosing should be closely linked to the presence of residual Fe3<sup>+</sup> in the filtered water and its turbidity. While physical models for two welldefined charged particles in an aqueous environment are ubiquitous ([Israelachvili,](#page-7-18) [2011;](#page-7-18) [Smith, Borkovec, & Trefalt,](#page-8-6) [2020\)](#page-8-6), the physical model of this specific process is inherently convoluted due to the myriad interactions between suspended solids in an ever fluctuating aqueous environment ([Dishon, Zohar, & Sivan,](#page-7-19) [2009;](#page-7-19) [Israelachvili](#page-7-18), [2011;](#page-7-18) [Misra, de Souza, Blankschtein, & Bazant](#page-7-20), [2019;](#page-7-20) [Pillai, Santana,](#page-7-21) [Das, Shrestha, Manalastas, & Mishra,](#page-7-21) [2020;](#page-7-21) [Ridwan, Shrestha, Mahar](#page-7-22)[jan, & Mishra,](#page-7-22) [2022](#page-7-22); [Smith et al.,](#page-8-6) [2020;](#page-8-6) [Smith, Maroni, Trefalt, &](#page-8-7) [Borkovec](#page-8-7), [2019](#page-8-7); [Trefalt, Behrens, & Borkovec](#page-8-8), [2016](#page-8-8)). Hence, a simple yet accurate technique to optimize this is a rarity and requires meticulous development.

In recent years, a number of studies have utilized algorithms to optimize the coagulation process including the use of fuzzy dynamic modeling [\(Bello, Hamam, & Djouani,](#page-7-23) [2014\)](#page-7-23), artificial neural networks (ANNs) ([Al-Abri, Al Anezi, Dakheel, & Hilal,](#page-7-24) [2010](#page-7-24); [Kennedy, Gandomi,](#page-7-25) [& Miller](#page-7-25), [2015\)](#page-7-25), and hybrid methods ([Zangooei, Delnavaz, & Asadol](#page-8-9)[lahfardi](#page-8-9), [2016a;](#page-8-9) [Zangooei, Delnavaz, Asadollahfardi, & Technology](#page-8-10), [2016b\)](#page-8-10). However, to our knowledge, there has not been any large-scale demonstrations of such algorithms and further studies on economic impacts of such optimizations. In light of the aforementioned, in the present study, we utilize an artificial neural network-based algorithm to optimize the chemical consumption of coagulants. The developed model is used to predict the Silt Density Index (SDI) of filtered seawater based on the coagulant dosing rate. SDI is a common measurement of the fouling potential of particles in reverse osmosis feedwater ([Wei,](#page-8-11) [Laborie, Aim, & Amy,](#page-8-11) [2012](#page-8-11)), and is a key indicator of the efficiency of the filtration and coagulation processes. In the beginning, the model is developed and trained using plant data from one of the biggest SWRO desalination plants in the Middle East. Before the development, the specific seawater parameters as inputs were modified by feature engineering and selected based on the feature selection technique and our domain knowledge. We then conduct a parametric optimization for artificial neural network architecture to achieve the highest accuracy between the predicted and test data sets. The model then is deployed and utilized in day-to-day operation in the respective plant. Further, the economic impact of the coagulant optimization is calculated by quantifying the operational cost saving for one-year operation.

## *2.1. Input and output parameter selection*

Seawater can be described by several physical properties. Relationship between coagulation process, coagulant chemical, and seawater parameters are rather well known. In general, many studies utilize turbidity, temperature, conductivity, and pH to correlate to coagulation ([Taloba,](#page-8-16) [2022\)](#page-8-16), even electrical conductivity of untreated water are used [\(Heddam,](#page-7-35) [2021](#page-7-35)). The models were developed using several water quality variables selected as input of the models, namely, turbidity, pH, dissolved oxygen, electrical conductivity, and the water temperature. In addition to that, Kim et al. utilize turbidity, temperature, pH, conductivity, historical coagulant dosage to estimate the coagulant dosage [\(Kim & Parnichkun](#page-7-36), [2017a](#page-7-36)). Zhang et al. used pH, turbidity, and temperature ([Zhang et al.](#page-8-15), [2013](#page-8-15)). Both resulted in optimization of coagulant dosing rate. In Wu & Lo's work, they utilize the coagulant dosage of the two preceding days, temperature, turbidity, color, pH, coagulation/flocculation, sedimentation and treated water to predict present coagulant dosing ([Wu & Lo,](#page-8-12) [2008](#page-8-12)). It is interesting to note that these studies rarely employ SDI for predicting or coagulant dosage reduction criteria. In our operational experience, SDI is the most reliable measurement of filtrate quality and criterial for optimum coagulant dosing and widely used by off-taker and membrane supplier for filtrate water specifications. In addition, these methods utilize Pearson correlation coefficient to select the appropriate input parameters. To this end, we utilize Pearson correlation and using wider range of parameters of respective time and historical data to forecast SDI to minimize coagulant consumption.

## 3. Methods

## 3.1. Desalination plant description

The desalination plant with production capacity of 216,000 m<sup>3</sup>/day is located in the southern part of Saudi Arabia. The desalination plant delivers potable water as product, thereby equipped with potabilization facilities (Fig. 1). In the intake section, the seawater is pumped by the intake pump and intermittently disinfected by dosing Sodium Hypochlorite (NaOCl). Subsequently, the seawater is treated with coagulant and flocculant during the coagulation process before entering the dual media filter (DMF). The seawater then is filtered using a microncartridge filter to remove the micron-scale particle. To meet contractual obligations the water reaching the reverse osmosis membrane should have a SDI of less than four. Before passing the seawater to the reverse osmosis section, the seawater is acidified and de-chlorinated. The second pass of the desalination plant starts with sodium hydroxide dosing to maximize boron removal. In the post-treatment process, the permeate water is treated with limewater and CO<sub>2</sub> for remineralization and sodium hydroxide for pH correction.

## 3.2. Data description

The raw dataset used in this model development comprises of several seawater variables including turbidity, pH, SDI before and after cartridge filter, and coagulant (FeCl<sub>3</sub>) and flocculant (polymer) dosing rates (see Table 1). The algorithm was trained using three years operational data from the SWRO desalination plant, consisting of 9261 values per parameter. Initially, the data was checked, cleaned, formatted, and validated (including removal of wrong/malformed date format and invalid data such as missing data points). We consider SDI to be valid if the value is above 0, and the polymer dosing rate below

1 ppm. Further, to better understand the relation between SDI and other parameters, we derived additional parameters based on the data. We add additional parameters based on the initial dataset as written below:

- (i) Shifting the next value to the current value  $([SDI]_{next,i} = [SDI]_{i+1}, [FeCl_3]_{next,i} = [FeCl_3]_{i+1}, [Polymer]_{next,i} = [Polymer]_{i+1})$
- (ii) Shifting the previous value to the current value  $([SDI]_{prev,i} = [SDI]_{i-1}, [FeCl_3]_{prev,i} = [FeCl_3]_{i-1}, [Polymer]_{prev,i} = [Polymer]_{i-1}, [Tur]_{prev,i} = [Tur]_{i-1}, [pH]_{prev,i} = [pH]_{i-1})$
- (iii) The differential of the next value  $(\Delta[SDI]_{next,i} = [SDI]_{next,i} [SDI]_i$ ,  $\Delta[FeCl_3]_{next,i} = [FeCl_3]_{next,i} [FeCl_3]_i$ )
- (iv) The differential of the previous value  $(\Delta[SDI]_{prev,i} = [SDI]_i [SDI]_{prev,i}, \Delta[FeCl_3]_{prev,i} = [FeCl_3]_i [FeCl_3]_{prev,i}, \Delta[Polymer]_{prev,i}$   $= [Polymer]_i [Polymer]_{prev,i}, \Delta[Tur]_{prev,i} = [Tur]_i [Tur]_{prev,i}, \Delta[pH]_{prev,i} = [pH]_i [pH]_{prev,i})$

We then perform feature selection to select the best subset of input parameters that correlates better to the output variable. This step, feature selection, is necessary to reduce computation time, improve learning accuracy, avoid redundancy, and provide a better understanding of data and model by removing irrelevant parameters which can lead to poor generalization (Domashova, Emtseva, Fail, & Gridin, 2021); (Orr & Müller, 1998). There are many methods for feature selection, and the widely known are filter, wrapper, and correlation-based methods (Chandrashekar & Sahin, 2014). The filter method is ranking the parameters relative to their significance to the predictor, while the wrapper method wraps the predictor on the search algorithm and provides a group of parameters that gives the highest accuracy. Here, we utilize the correlation-based method-pairwise Pearson correlation (Hall, 1999; Pearson, 1909). This correlation measures the linear relationship between two variables. The Pearson coefficient ranges from −1 to +1, where 0 value indicates no correlation between two variables. Both -1 and +1 are indicating that the two variables correlate perfectly linear with each other (Benesty, Chen, Huang, & Cohen, 2009).

Combining our domain knowledge and evaluation of the pairwise Pearson correlation of those parameters, we selected specific parameters for SDI prediction (Fig. 2). Based on the data and additional calculated parameters, we determine 7 parameters as inputs. The inputs are previous SDI, previous seawater pH, actual seawater pH, previous turbidity, actual turbidity, previous ferric chloride dose, and proposed ferric chloride dose. After selecting and normalizing the subset of input parameters, we split the data for training and testing. We allocate 90% of the data for training and 10% for testing.

## 3.3. Artificial neural network architecture definition

Artificial neural networks are bioinspired algorithms built based on the human brain which contains connected neurons or neural networks (Orr & Müller, 1998). Each layer contains a single or multiple node(s) which is called Neuron. Inside the Neuron, there is a function to perform a mathematical operation. We specifically construct the artificial neural network using a dense layer. The dense layer is the term when the node is connected to all neurons in the preceding layer. In our model development, we utilize the sequential model to construct a layer-by-layer model. Sequential models are linear stacks of layers where one-layer leads to the next. The sequential API is straightforward to implement and only requires the previous layer's output as the input to the next layer (Chollet, 2020), April 12). In

other words, Sequential API uses a plain stack of the layer where each layer has one input and output tensor which cannot share layers. We create one input layer followed by four hidden layers. The input layer takes in the data and pre-processes it. The hidden layer performs a nonlinear transformation of input and the output layer takes the results from the hidden layer, transforms them, and gives the final output. To optimize the architecture of our artificial neural network, we adjust several different parameters such as the number of hidden layers, epoch, activation function, optimizer, and batch size.

The input and hidden layers are utilizing a non-linear rectified linear unit (ReLU) as an activation function, where the output is using linear activation function:  $O_i = F(w \cdot x_i + b)$ , where F represents activation function, w represents the weight data, and b represents a biased value used in machine learning to optimize the model (Nair & Hinton, 2010). To evaluate the model during the training, we employ the mean squared error as the loss function. We further applied the optimization function that optimizes the weights of input based on prediction and the loss function, called adaptive moment estimation (Adam). Adam is a first-order gradient algorithm based on adaptive estimates of lower-order moments (Kingma Diederik & Adam, 2014; Reddi, Kale, & Kumar, 2019). To evaluate the performance of the model after training, we used mean average error,  $MAE = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} |y_i - x_i|$ , and mean square error,  $MSE = \left(\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\right)$ , where *n* is number of sample,  $y_i$  and  $x_i$  are predicted value and actual value, and  $\sigma^2$  is variance. The code for the developed model is provided in supplemental information (see Table 2).

## *4.1. Artificial neural network forecasting results*

There are several factors affecting the quality of ANNs output including its architecture. One main issue with ANNs is overfitting, where the model is memorizing the dataset instead of creating generalization ([Domashova et al.,](#page-7-39) [2021\)](#page-7-39). Typically, the higher the epoch, the higher the accuracy. However, if the epoch is too high, the accuracy starts to fall, which indicates overfitting. To address this issue, we conduct a parametric study on the neural-network architecture. We sequentially change epoch, batch size, number of input and hidden layers, and their corresponding nodes. The optimal result is achieved after the training with 500 epochs and batch size of 50. The input layer consists of 10 nodes and 4 hidden layers with 256, 128, 64, and 64 nodes, respectively ([Fig.](#page-4-1) [3\)](#page-4-1).

In the artificial neural network model based on the architectural definition above, we achieved 95.33% accuracy ([Fig.](#page-5-0) [4](#page-5-0)). This case yields sufficiently minimal mean absolute error (MAE) and mean squared error (MSE), the value of 0.15 and 0.047 respectively. The predicted SDI and actual SDI comparison using this model is deployed in our simulation platform and the corresponding plot is shown below. The high accuracy of testing data indicates that the model can draw the general trend between the input parameters and the output.

To use the algorithm, the user is required to input the feed seawater SDI, pH, turbidity, and previous and proposed coagulant dosing concentration. The user will obtain the predicted value of SDI. Based on this value, the user will decide if they would like to apply the proposed coagulant dosing in the operation or further optimize it.

# *4.2. Comparative performance with different models*

We compare the ANNs model's accuracy with that of three distinct machine learning algorithms: decision tree regression, random forest regressor, and linear regression.
