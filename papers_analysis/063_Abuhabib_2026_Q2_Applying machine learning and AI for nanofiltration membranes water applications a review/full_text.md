#### ABSTRACT

Nanofiltration (NF) has long been the focus of researchers and operators in the field of water and wastewater treatment and desalination. Characterised by both high rejection of diverse substances depending on their molecular composition and charge, and high flux, NF membranes have demonstrated broad applicability while maintaining long-lasting performance. However, NF membrane scalability and reliability are intrinsically constrained by non-linear phenomena like complex solute-rejection mechanisms, long-term fouling dynamics, and the inherent flux–selectivity trade-off. Traditional mechanistic models relying on simplifying assumptions within the solution–diffusion framework, often fail to accurately predict performance in heterogeneous, real-time water matrices, leading to a critical divergence between predictive capability and operational reality. This review addresses this gap by comprehensively assessing the integration of machine learning (ML) and artificial intelligence (AI) across three critical domains: fabrication optimisation, performance prediction, and fouling diagnosis and mitigation, through the analysis of 100 NF-ML publications over the past 10 years. Additionally, it highlights key methodological challenges including data scarcity and heterogeneity, and lack of robust external validation. Finally, the review emphasises that future advancements lie in reinforcement learning for real-time adaptive control and in hybrid ML-mechanistic frameworks bridging the existing gap between datadriven prediction and transparent mechanistic understanding.

Key words: machine learning, membrane, nanofiltration, water applications

#### HIGHLIGHTS

- Illustration of machine learning (ML) approaches and algorithms applicable to nanofiltration (NF) membranes.
- Explanation of inputs, outputs, and processes in between, all of which act as a roadmap on how ML flow looks for NF membrane processes and applications.
- Illustration of challenges and solutions for dataset availability and processing, and ML models' accuracy and interpretability.

#### 1. INTRODUCTION

Driven by population growth and environmental stress, the need for reliable freshwater is currently exceeding global supply capacities. Projections estimate that two-thirds of the world's population will soon face water insecurity. However, these figures reveal only a fraction of the broader systematic challenges in water management.

However, these figures alone reveal only part of the challenge. More critically, they underscore the inadequacy of current treatment strategies and the urgent need for sustainable, high-efficiency purification technologies capable of addressing complex and variable water quality ([Isawi 2023](#page-27-0); [El-Aassar](#page-26-0) et al. 2024). In this context, membrane-based desalination and water treatment have transitioned from supplementary options to central components of global water security frameworks. Beyond

However, despite their widespread deployment, significant performance trade-offs remain across membrane classes, especially regarding fouling behaviour, selectivity/permeability limitations, and cost-efficiency balances [\(Isawi 2018](#page-27-0), [2023\)](#page-27-0). Several membrane-based water treatment methods, such as ultrafiltration (UF) [\(Isawi 2019](#page-27-0)), nanofiltration (NF) (Di et al. Among these, as a pressure-driven process characterised by a semi-porous structure, NF serves as an intermediary technology bridging the gap between UF and RO. These membranes typically feature effective pore diameters in the sub-nanometer range and a molecular weight cut-off (MWCO) of 0.2–10 kDa [\(Behroozi & Ataabadi 2021;](#page-26-0) [Meng](#page-28-0) et al. 2023; [Usha](#page-30-0) et al. 2025). Due to this intermediate selectivity, NF operates at significantly lower operational pressure than RO while achieving higher permeation flux. Consequently, NF has emerged as a highly attractive solution for diverse water applications including brackish water desalination, wastewater polishing, and seawater pre-treatment. Having too much in common in terms properties and selectivity as a pressure-driven process, both RO and NF have high similarity when it comes to water applications. Practically, NF membranes are highly regarded for their robust capacity to selectively separate inorganic salts and low MW organic molecules from aqueous solutions. These systems occupy a distinct operational niche, offering separation efficiencies and transport characteristics that intermediate between the coarser UF and the more-dense RO processes. Unlike RO, NF membranes reject monovalent ions at a lower rate, while effectively rejecting divalent ions at a higher rate and maintaining high

Although NF research has a long history extending back several decades, the analysis in this review intentionally focuses on the most recent 10-year period, during which the acceleration of NF research has been particularly. The past 10 years witnessed growing interest in exploring and investigating NF membranes applications as indicated by the significant number of publications found in Scopus database [\(Figure 1\)](#page-1-0) where the number of publications increased from 432 in 2015 to 1,332 in 2025. Out of membrane water-based publications (5,592 in 2015 and 11,587 in 2025), NF membranes witnessed an increase in percentage of publications from 7.7% in 2015 to 11.5% in 2025. When machine learning (ML) included in the search for both NF membranes and membranes in general for water-based applications, both witnessed significant increase over the same period with NF-ML-based publications represents 10% out of total membrane-ML-based publications. NF membranes have been increasingly applied in complex water and wastewater matrices, where performance is governed by a non-linear interplay of membrane structural properties, feed composition, operating conditions, and fouling dynamics [\(Boussouga](#page-26-0) et al. 2022). These systems are often difficult to describe adequately with purely mechanistic models, especially when many variables interact simultaneously and when data are heterogeneous or scarce (Du [et al.](#page-26-0) 2022). The rapid expansion of ML and broader artificial intelligence (AI) frameworks has provided powerful data-driven methodologies for capturing complex non-linear relationships within membrane systems. These computational tools effectively support both the predictive modelling of key performance indicators, such as flux, rejection, and fouling indices, and the inverse design of membrane structures and optimal operating conditions (Li et al. [2023\)](#page-28-0). Recent reviews have discussed AI and ML in membrane technology at a general level, typically combining UF, NF, RO, and organic solvent nanofiltration (OSN) within a single framework.

This review aims to comprehensively examine the advancements and progress in applying ML to NF membrane research. This is supported by a particular focus on key thematic areas such as predictive modelling of membrane fabrication and surface modification, performance optimisation through data-driven characterisation, and ML-enabled solutions to address membrane fouling. It further explores the role of ML in expanding industrial and environmental applications of NF membranes. Such exploration is done by identifying emerging trends, knowledge gaps, available models and algorithms and their applicability, and future research directions that could accelerate the integration of ML in NF membrane innovation and promote their wider adoption across sectors.

# 3. OVERVIEW OF NF MEMBRANE TECHNOLOGY

# 3.1. NF membrane separation fundamentals and applications

NF membrane is primarily a separation process in which an aqueous solution is driven through by applied pressure. Certain substances are rejected at different rates, influenced by a variety of factors including the geometry and charge of the membrane surface, as well as the ionic composition and charge of the solution. NF membranes are crafted with a specific MWCO that permits the retention of certain solutes while allowing others to flow through. In addition, the significant influence of the NF membranes filtration process by hydrophobic and hydrophilic forces interactions contributes to solute size exclusion and the Donan effect and attributing to enabling the targeted removal of divalent ions, small organic compounds, and viruses from water solutions ([Donnan 1995](#page-26-0); Li et al. [2022\)](#page-28-0). Practically, solute transport across the NF membrane occurs through three primary mechanisms: convection, diffusion, and electromigration. Convection occurs in a thick, highly porous membrane with high permeate flux. Diffusion takes place in a thin membrane having a high surface charge density with low porosity and permeate flux. Electromigration is directly linked to a moderate charge density at the membrane surface while a solute passes through. Furthermore, hydrophobic and hydrophilic interactions mechanisms are influential to the uncharged solutes adsorption and rejection depending on these solutes hydrophobic or hydrophilic properties. For example, greater adsorption on hydrophobic membrane surfaces occurs due to hydrophobic interactions, leading to have higher rejection rates ([Wang](#page-30-0) et al. 2021, [2024e](#page-30-0)), while surface charge and pore size influence hydrophilic interactions depending on the solute size and polarity [\(Saeid Rostami & Mehdi Khodaei 2023;](#page-29-0) Wang et al. [2024e\)](#page-30-0). These key performance features and mechanisms have earned NF membranes wide applicability in many water industries. They have been widely used and applied in various industries, most of which water applications including water desalination and treatment ([Voutchkov](#page-30-0) [2018;](#page-30-0) [Belhamidi](#page-26-0) et al. 2022; [Mallya](#page-28-0) et al. 2023; [El-Ghzizel](#page-26-0) et al. 2024; [Idrissi](#page-27-0) et al. 2024; [Laurell](#page-28-0) et al. 2024), wastewater treatment ([Samavati](#page-29-0) et al. 2023, [2024](#page-29-0); [Al Kholif](#page-26-0) et al. 2024), and food industry ([Abdorrezaee & Raisi 2021;](#page-25-0) [Roselli](#page-29-0) et al. [2025\)](#page-29-0). The major focus on NF membranes application is commonly the water applications with some interest on other non-water applications whereby NF membranes are applied in the form of OSN [\(Alqadhi](#page-26-0) et al. 2024; [Waheed](#page-30-0) et al. 2024; [Roselli](#page-29-0) et al. 2025).

#### 3.2. Traditional challenges in NF membrane design and operation

NF membrane design and operation, as well as performance modelling, remain at the focus of membrane manufacturers and researchers. Consequently, at the heart of such focus is having an NF membrane that fits certain uses with desirable features and sustainably maintained performance with respect to an acceptable operational condition [\(Zahid](#page-30-0) et al. 2018; [Usha](#page-30-0) et al. [2025\)](#page-30-0). On the other hand, fouling continues to rank as the number one challenge in membrane technology in general, and NF membrane is no exception. It can also be seen as the performance turning point where deterioration is observed, and symptoms begin to surface, obviously indicated by a decline in flux (recovery reduction). This is where energy consumption starts to increase, and maintenance and cleaning become unusually more frequent. It is hard to tell when the fouling would occur, but mostly, it will take some time until it becomes noticeable, as the membrane performance or basically flux declining may occur gradually or at a very minor magnitude. Despite the massive number of studies and research conducted to understand membrane fouling and the way to control or mitigate it, the need to further understand and explore the phenomena of how it happens and parameters affecting or contributing to it, as well as controlling measures are still vivid and growing [\(Al Kholif](#page-26-0) et al. 2024; [Borgomeo 2024;](#page-26-0) Liu [et al.](#page-28-0) 2024). Fundamentally, concentration polarisation and osmotic pressure are potentially the phenomenal mechanisms involved in fouling. In concentration polarisation, the retained solutes are accumulated in the membrane boundary on the feed side leading to form high solute concentration on the membrane surface. On the other hand, the osmatic pressure increasing because of accumulated solutes (organic or inorganic), which will consequently lead to the deterioration of the transmembrane pressure and permeate flux ([Mohammad](#page-28-0) et al. 2015). In general, NF membrane fouling can be categorised based on the type that is linked to the foulant species causing the fouling. Practically, there are several major factors affecting or influencing NF membranes fouling that can be summarised as ([Zheng](#page-31-0) et al. [2024b](#page-31-0)):

- Raw water/feed characteristics (natural organic matter (NOM) concentration, ionic interaction, pH, and temperature).
- Membrane characteristics (morphology, structure, hydrophilicity, and other surface properties).
- Operational conditions (pressure, recovery rate, and hydrodynamic conditions).

Given the fact that membrane fouling is inevitable, mitigation and control are essential to reduce the negative effects caused by fouling to maintain membrane performance and reserve membrane lifespan. Based on the illustration made above for factors influencing membrane fouling, mitigation measures can be suggested to deal with these factors accordingly (Liu *et al.* 2024; Zheng *et al.* 2024b). For instance, controlling and optimising feed water and parameters operational conditions, as well as pre-treatment methods, could be the key to reducing fouling effectively. In addition, the use of some materials and additives that can increase membrane fouling resistance in fabrication or surface modification processes may also reduce fouling tendency as preventive measures.

#### 3.3. NF membrane modelling

NF membranes modelling plays a crucial role in deepening theoretical understanding, guiding effective maintenance strategies, and driving performance improvements (Mohammad et al. 2015; Al Kholif et al. 2024; Petukhov & Johnson 2024; Xu et al. 2024). Previously, the approach has evolved from empirical black-box models rooted in irreversible thermodynamics to models based on the extended Nernst-Planck equation (Tsuru et al. 1991; Levenstein et al. 1996). The latter has gained prominence due to its ability to provide insights into the properties of both the membrane and the process stream. Such models aim at integrating as much physical realism as possible into the membrane processes representation to enable a closer alignment between measurable quantities and adjustable model parameters. However, reliance on pore radius and membrane charge on characteristic parameters significantly limited NF modelling, as they are difficult to measure at the near-atomic length scales. The highly complex neutral solutes and charged electrolytes are deeply governed by nanoscale phenomena, posting a rigorous challenge to any macroscopic continuum model of ion transport and partitioning. Similarly, molecular dynamics simulations with more precise physical descriptions are constrained by the physical structure understanding which is highly limited to real NF membranes electrical properties and streams. Consequently, advancements in NF modelling have progressed alongside improvements in measurement techniques for characterising NF membranes and process streams. These enhanced techniques are critical for validating the appropriateness of model parameters, ensuring that the models can effectively represent the intricate phenomena occurring within NF systems. Ion transportation is mathematically described by the extended equation of Nernst-Planck (Equation (1)), while Equation (2) is the full extended Nernst-Planck that can be stated as the ion transportation in a solution governing equation, practically describing ion transportation into membrane pore similar to the one observed for NF and RO membranes (Giddings et al. 1968; Deen 1987; Donnan 1995).

$$J_i = -\frac{c_i K_{i,d} D_{i,\infty}}{RT} \frac{d\mu}{dx} + K_{i,c} c_i V \tag{1}$$

$$j_{i} = -D_{i,p} \frac{dc_{i}}{dx} \frac{c_{i}D_{i,p}}{\gamma_{i}} \frac{d\gamma_{i}}{dx} - \frac{c_{i}D_{i,p}}{RT} V_{si} \frac{dP}{dx} - \frac{c_{i}D_{i,p}}{RT} z_{i} F \frac{d\varphi}{dx} + K_{i,c} c_{i} V$$

$$\tag{2}$$

where  $j_i$  is the ionic flux, c is the concentration, V is the solvent velocity, and  $K_{i,c}$  and  $K_{i,d}$  are hindrance factors to account for the convection and diffusion inside a confined space.

#### 4. AI, ML, AND DL: AN OVERVIEW

#### 4.1. Al and ML concepts and algorithms

ML can be broadly understood as a data-driven framework in which computational models learn relationships between inputs and outputs by optimising performance through experience, rather than relying solely on predefined physical equations (Mitchell 1997). In the context of NF research, ML is not employed to replace fundamental transport theory, but rather to complement it by capturing complex, non-linear interactions between membrane properties, operating conditions, and performance indicators such as flux, rejection, and fouling behaviour.

Unlike conventional hypothesis-driven modelling approaches, where system behaviour is inferred from simplified physical assumptions, ML enables direct learning from experimental or operational data when sufficient volume and quality are available (Bishop & Nasrabadi 2006; Madhavan 2025). This paradigm shift has been accelerated by advances in computational

power and the increasing availability of membrane performance data, allowing ML models to approximate highly non-linear phenomena that are difficult to describe using classical mechanistic formulations. However, this flexibility often comes at the cost of reduced interpretability, necessitating careful validation and, increasingly, the use of explainable-AI tools.

It should be emphasised that the application of ML in NF research remains constrained by practical limitations. Most published studies rely on relatively small, heterogeneous datasets and simplified performance metrics, which restrict model generalisability and the extent to which ML can yield robust mechanistic insight. Moreover, data-driven approaches in membrane science have not yet been fully supported by standardised practices for data curation, external validation, and reporting. Consequently, while ML has demonstrated strong predictive capability in NF applications, its role should presently be viewed as a complementary tool that enhances understanding and optimisation, rather than as a standalone substitute for physically grounded modelling frameworks (Figure 2) ([Ignacz](#page-27-0) et al. 2025).

The diversity of ML models provides NF researchers with a flexible toolbox for addressing different data structures, prediction targets, and modelling objectives. Model selection in NF studies is therefore primarily governed by the nature of available data (e.g., static vs. time-series, laboratory vs. operational datasets), the required level of predictive accuracy, and the need for interpretability in relation to membrane transport and fouling mechanisms. Accordingly, ML approaches in NF are commonly categorised based on their learning paradigm, such as supervised, unsupervised, and reinforcement learning (RL). The following sections focus specifically on these model categories and their practical application in membrane science, with emphasis on NF membranes.

Despite the rapid expansion of ML applications in NF research, several constraints remain evident. Most published studies rely on limited and heterogeneous datasets, often derived from single laboratories or narrowly defined operating conditions, which restrict model robustness and generalisability. In addition, the transition towards data-driven modelling has not yet been matched by standardised practices for data curation, external validation, and transparent reporting in membrane research. Consequently, while ML has demonstrated strong predictive capability in NF applications, its ability to deliver broadly transferable mechanistic insight and plant-scale reliability remains only partially realised.

# 4.2. AI in membrane science

AI has become an important enabler of data-driven modelling in environmental and membrane engineering. Within membrane science, ML and DL are the most relevant AI branches, as they provide predictive and optimisation frameworks capable of capturing the highly non-linear behaviour of membrane processes (Cao [et al.](#page-26-0) 2024; [Shahouni](#page-29-0) et al. 2024). In NF systems, performance is governed by complex interactions among membrane properties, operating conditions, and solute characteristics, which often challenge classical modelling approaches. Conventional theories such as the solution–diffusion model (SDM) and Donnan exclusion principles offer valuable mechanistic insight but frequently fail to provide accurate predictions across diverse water matrices and operating regimes. AI-based models offer a complementary advantage by learning system behaviour directly from data, enabling the identification of non-linear patterns that are difficult to represent using simplified physical assumptions ([Ignacz](#page-27-0) et al. 2023a). Whereas traditional NF studies have relied heavily on semi-empirical correlations derived under controlled laboratory conditions, ML models trained on heterogeneous datasets have demonstrated improved robustness when extrapolated to variable feed compositions and dynamic operating environments. For example, data-driven models have shown superior capability in predicting flux decline and fouling trajectories under fluctuating feed conditions, where mechanistic fouling models often lose validity (Hu [et al.](#page-27-0) 2025; [Singh](#page-29-0) et al. 2025). For example, whereas mechanistic fouling models often collapse under highly variable feed conditions, ML models trained on diverse datasets can successfully predict flux decline trajectories. In a broader sense, AI can be described as a form of behavioural science of machines. Just as behavioural science studies human decision-making under uncertainty, AI models exhibit learning behaviours shaped by feedback. In supervised learning, models behave by adjusting internal weights in response to labelled outcomes. In RL, the behaviour is refined by rewards and penalties, mirroring human trial-and-error learning (Cao [et al.](#page-26-0) 2024). This behavioural analogy is particularly useful in NF research: membranes themselves respond dynamically to their environment (fouling, scaling, chemical changes), and AI provides a behavioural mirror – adapting predictions in real time as system conditions evolve.

Recent studies increasingly position AI within the broader digitalisation of water treatment systems. [Shahouni](#page-29-0) et al. (2024) highlighted the role of AI in predictive modelling and membrane material discovery, while Cao et al. [\(2024\)](#page-26-0) emphasised its integration into digital twin frameworks for membrane plants. In NF applications, AI-enabled digital twins provide dynamic representations of real systems, allowing prediction of flux, rejection, and fouling behaviour, as well as optimisation of cleaning and operational strategies. Practical applications demonstrate the maturity of these approaches: [Zhang](#page-30-0) et al. (2021) applied deep representation learning to predict NF flux and rejection under limited data availability. Ji et al. [\(2025\)](#page-28-0) employed artificial neural networks (ANNs) combined with explainable-AI tools to optimise NF performance for lithium recovery, and Gong et al. [\(2024\)](#page-27-0) developed hybrid DL models to accurately predict perfluorooctane sulfonate (PFOS) rejection while maintaining interpretability. Collectively, these studies illustrate that AI in NF research serves not only as a predictive tool but also as a means to elucidate performance-relevant behavioural patterns under complex and variable conditions.

The reviewed studies ([Table 2](#page-8-0)) collectively indicate that AI can enhance both predictive capability and process understanding in NF research; nevertheless, several NF-specific limitations remain. Most reported applications are confined to laboratory or pilot-scale systems, with model training commonly based on datasets derived from single studies or narrowly defined operating conditions. Such constraints limit model robustness and raise concerns regarding transferability to fullscale NF plants and to feedwaters with substantially different physicochemical characteristics.

Although explainable-AI techniques, such as SHAP, have improved the interpretation of data-driven models and provided insights into dominant NF performance drivers, their adoption remains inconsistent, and systematic benchmarking against established transport and fouling models is still limited. As a result, current AI applications in NF should be viewed as complementary tools that support performance prediction and optimisation, rather than as standalone replacements for physically grounded modelling frameworks.

# 4.3.1. Supervised learning

Supervised learning is the most widely adopted ML paradigm in NF research, as it directly links measurable inputs to performance outputs using labelled datasets. In NF applications, inputs typically include operating conditions (e.g., transmembrane pressure, pH, ionic composition, solute MW) and membrane properties (e.g., pore size, surface charge, hydrophilicity), while outputs represent performance indicators such as water flux, solute rejection, fouling indices, or energy consumption. Numerous studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of supervised learning for predicting NF performance. For example, Wang et al. [\(2023b\)](#page-30-0) applied extreme gradient boosting to predict haloacetic acid rejection, achieving high predictive accuracy while identifying dominant rejection drivers. Singh et al. [\(2025\)](#page-29-0) showed that ANNs outperformed other models in predicting PFOS rejection, whereas random forest (RF) models provided valuable interpretability through variable-importance analysis. Similarly, Hu et al. [\(2025\)](#page-27-0) employed supervised models to predict pharmaceutical compound rejection and to elucidate the relative roles of size exclusion and electrostatic interactions.

Despite these successes, supervised learning in NF remains constrained by data availability and quality. Model performance is strongly dependent on the representativeness of labelled datasets, which are often limited by experimental cost and laboratory-specific protocols. Moreover, validation is frequently based on random data splits from single datasets, offering limited evidence of external validity. Without systematic testing across independent datasets or different NF systems, the generalisability of supervised models remains uncertain.

# 4.3.2. Unsupervised learning

Unsupervised learning aims to identify hidden structures and patterns in data without relying on labelled outputs. This paradigm is particularly relevant for NF research when dealing with high-dimensional water quality data, membrane characterisation datasets, or long-term operational records. Although less frequently applied than supervised learning, recent studies highlight its potential value in NF-related investigations. Ignacz et al. [\(2023a\)](#page-27-0) demonstrated that clustering and dimensionality reduction techniques can reveal meaningful groupings of membrane–solute interactions in OSN, a concept transferable to aqueous NF systems. Niu et al. [\(2025\)](#page-29-0) applied principal component analysis to characterise dissolved organic matter (DOM) affecting NF fouling, identifying key components that explained the majority of data variance and simplifying subsequent modelling efforts.

The primary strength of unsupervised learning in NF lies in its exploratory capability, enabling identification of dominant patterns, operational regimes, or membrane performance clusters. However, because it does not provide direct performance predictions, unsupervised learning is most effective when used in combination with supervised approaches. At present, many unsupervised NF studies remain exploratory, with limited integration into predictive or control frameworks. In addition, results are often sensitive to data pre-processing choices and clustering metrics, underscoring the need for more rigorous evaluation of stability and physical interpretability before such insights are used to guide membrane design or operation.

# 4.3.3. Reinforcement learning

RL represents a distinct paradigm in ML, where an agent interacts with an environment and improves its decision-making through rewards and penalties. Unlike supervised learning, which requires labelled input–output pairs, RL is well-suited for sequential optimisation tasks and real-time process control. In the field of membrane technologies, RL has only recently been introduced, yet several pioneering studies demonstrate its considerable potential. [Bonny](#page-26-0) et al. (2022) presented one of the earliest applications of deep RL for RO desalination. Their study developed a deep RL-based control system capable of dynamically adjusting operating parameters such as feed flow and pressure to maximise water recovery while minimising energy consumption. The system demonstrated superior adaptability compared with fixed-parameter control strategies, effectively handling variations in feedwater salinity and demonstrating the feasibility of RL in real-time process optimisation.

Future research will therefore need to focus on the design of safe, sample-efficient RL schemes and their integration into regulatory-compliant digital twin frameworks.

#### 4.3.4. Comparative perspective

Taken together, the three categories of ML – supervised, unsupervised, and RL serve complementary and increasingly synergistic roles in NF research. Supervised learning remains the dominant paradigm, excelling in predictive modelling when sufficient labelled data is available, as demonstrated in studies of PFOS rejection, pharmaceutical removal, and HAA behaviour. Unsupervised learning, while less widely applied, provides an important exploratory function by uncovering hidden patterns in complex datasets, revealing clusters of solutes with similar rejection profiles or operational regimes associated with fouling risk. RL, although still largely conceptual, offers an adaptive layer capable of enabling real-time optimisation within digital twin frameworks by learning from continuous feedback. Recent reviews stress that the true power of ML in NF does not lie in these paradigms in isolation, but rather in their integration. A realistic future scenario is one in which unsupervised learning is used to classify and reduce complexity in large datasets, supervised models then predict key outputs such as rejection or fouling under identified regimes, and RL adaptively fine-tunes operational strategies in real time. Such a layered approach mirrors human learning behaviours, combining classification, prediction, and adaptation, and resonates strongly with the adaptive response of NF membranes themselves to dynamic environments. The implication is that NF research is moving towards not just isolated predictive models, but towards comprehensive, self-learning frameworks that integrate multiple ML paradigms into a single intelligent system.

#### 4.4. ML algorithms and interpretability studies

ML algorithms are the backbone of data-driven research in NF. Their success depends not only on predictive accuracy but also on robustness, interpretability, and applicability across diverse datasets. In recent years, NF literature has seen a wide spectrum of ML algorithms, ranging from regression-based models to advanced ensemble methods and neural networks. Each of these has demonstrated strengths and limitations depending on dataset size, quality, and complexity.

#### 4.4.1. Regression-based models

Regression-based methods represent the earliest ML applications in NF research. They establish mathematical relationships between input variables (e.g., pressure, solute concentration, pH) and output performance metrics (e.g., flux, rejection). Their simplicity provides interpretability, but their inability to model non-linear interactions often limits predictive accuracy.

Singh et al. [\(2025\)](#page-29-0) compared linear regression and ridge regression with advanced models such as RF and ANN. Using datasets of PFOS rejection under varying pressures (2–8 bar), pH values, and ionic compositions, they observed that linear regression achieved only moderate predictive power (R<sup>2</sup> ≈ 0.78). Ridge regression slightly improved the fit by penalising large coefficients but still lagged non-linear models. The authors concluded that regression methods could identify general trends, such as the positive effect of higher pressures on rejection, but failed to capture interaction effects such as the combined influence of divalent cations and pH on PFOS rejection.

Ignacz et al. [\(2023a\)](#page-27-0) also tested regression models in their work on OSN and highlighted similar shortcomings. While regression models were interpretable and aligned with solution–diffusion theory, they consistently underestimated flux under conditions of high solute loading, demonstrating that linear assumptions break down in highly non-linear systems. Regression models remain useful for establishing baselines and for situations where interpretability is prioritised. Their ability to clearly show linear relationships makes them valuable for educational or initial exploration stages. However, the NF literature consistently demonstrates that regression methods underperform compared with non-linear ML methods, particularly for datasets with interacting operational and material variables. Overall, regression-based models retain value as transparent baselines and for generating first-order insights into variable relationships; however, their limitations are consistently exposed in NF applications involving strong non-linearity and interaction effects. The tendency of such models to underestimate performance at the extremes of operating ranges, and their inability to represent complex coupled phenomena such as simultaneous fouling and speciation changes, suggest that their role in modern NF research should be primarily supportive. In many cases, regression models are most useful when embedded within hybrid or physics-informed frameworks rather than used as standalone predictive tools.

# 4.4.2. Tree-based ensemble methods (RF, GBM, XGBoost)

Tree-based ensemble methods are among the most successful ML approaches applied in NF. Algorithms such as RF, gradient boosting machine (GBM), and extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost) combine multiple decision trees to improve predictive accuracy and reduce overfitting. Importantly, they also provide variable importance rankings, which offer mechanistic insights into NF processes. Figure 4 illustrates an ensembled learning approach.

Singh et al. [\(2025\)](#page-29-0) applied RF alongside ANN, SVM, and regression to predict PFOS rejection. RF models achieved R<sup>2</sup> ≈ 0.91, slightly lower than ANN but still strong. Importantly, RF identified pH and trivalent cations as the most influential parameters, aligning with experimental knowledge that PFOS rejection is highly sensitive to ionic composition. The authors

# 4.4.3. Artificial neural networks

ANNs are among the most widely applied ML tools in NF research because of their ability to approximate highly non-linear relationships between operational parameters, solute characteristics, and membrane performance.

Ji et al. [\(2025\)](#page-28-0) applied ANN models to predict lithium permeation and Li/Mg selectivity in NF membranes treating unconventional water sources. Their ANN achieved R<sup>2</sup> . 0.96, outperforming regression and RF models. Using Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP), they demonstrated that membrane MW cut-off and contact angle had greater influence on selectivity than operating parameters such as pressure. This result not only validated the ANN's predictions against physical intuition but also showcased how explainability tools can mitigate the 'black-box' perception of neural models.

[Zhang](#page-30-0) et al. (2021) employed deep representation learning, with ANN-based components, to model the performance of polyamide thin-film NF membranes. To address data scarcity, they used data augmentation strategies to artificially expand the dataset. Their ANN achieved mean relative errors below 5% for both flux and rejection, proving that even under limited data availability, ANN performance can be sustained when combined with augmentation techniques.

Hu et al. [\(2025\)](#page-27-0) tested ANN alongside RF and XGBoost for predicting PhAC rejection. ANN achieved R<sup>2</sup> values in the 0.92–0.95 range, comparable to XGBoost and superior to SVM. The model captured the importance of electrostatic interactions in rejection mechanisms, aligning with observed experimental results. However, the authors cautioned that ANN models are prone to overfitting with limited data, highlighting the need for careful regularisation.

Collectively, ANNs consistently deliver high predictive accuracy in NF studies, outperforming regression and often matching ensemble models. Their main weakness is overfitting and limited interpretability, but explainability frameworks such as SHAP and sensitivity analysis are increasingly adopted to improve trust in their predictions. While ANNs consistently deliver high predictive accuracy, their widespread use in NF research raises several methodological concerns. Overfitting remains a recurrent issue, particularly when complex architectures are trained on limited datasets that contain experimental noise or unbalanced operating conditions. Regularisation strategies and data augmentation are not always reported in sufficient detail, and uncertainty quantification is seldom undertaken. Furthermore, although explainable-AI tools have begun to reduce the black-box character of ANNs, the correspondence between attribution scores and true mechanistic causality is not always critically examined. Consequently, ANN-based findings should be interpreted cautiously unless supported by independent experimental validation or mechanistic reasoning.

#### 4.4.4. Support vector machines (SVMs)

SVMs are effective for high-dimensional and small-sample problems, but their use in NF has been limited compared with ANN and ensemble methods. Singh et al. [\(2025\)](#page-29-0) included SVM in their comparative analysis of PFOS rejection. The SVM achieved R<sup>2</sup> ≈ 0.82, which was lower than RF (0.91) and ANN (0.95). The authors suggested that SVM struggled with the non-linear interactions between pH, ionic strength, and pressure, which are common in NF. Hu et al. [\(2025\)](#page-27-0) also tested SVM for pharmaceutical rejection. Their results indicated that SVM achieved moderate performance (R<sup>2</sup> ≈ 0.85), but lagged behind XGBoost and ANN. They concluded that SVM could be valuable when datasets are small and simple, but it is less suitable for large, complex datasets with multiple interacting parameters. SVM has value as a benchmark model, especially in early-stage research or with limited datasets. However, NF literature shows a clear trend: as datasets grow in size and complexity, ensemble methods and ANN consistently outperform SVM in both accuracy and robustness. The comparative studies reviewed suggest that SVMs now occupy a secondary role in NF applications. Their advantages for small and moderately complex datasets are increasingly overshadowed by the flexibility of ensemble and neural network methods. Moreover, the sensitivity of SVM performance to kernel choice and hyperparameter tuning is rarely explored systematically, and the resulting models often provide limited mechanistic insight. In practice, SVMs function mainly as useful benchmarks rather than as methods of choice for state-of-the-art NF modelling, and future work may justifiably prioritise more interpretable or more scalable alternatives.

#### 4.4.5. Hybrid and ensemble frameworks

Hybrid and ensemble frameworks combine the strengths of multiple algorithms, improving accuracy, robustness, and sometimes interpretability. Ji et al. [\(2025\)](#page-28-0) integrated ANN with multi-objective optimisation to balance lithium permeation and Li/Mg selectivity. Their hybrid framework revealed trade-offs between high lithium recovery and maintaining selectivity, providing practical design insights for NF-based resource recovery. Wang et al. [\(2023b\)](#page-30-0) demonstrated that XGBoost, an ensemble boosting algorithm, outperformed ANN and SVM in predicting HAA rejection (R<sup>2</sup> . 0.95). Similarly, Hu et al. [\(2025\)](#page-27-0) confirmed that ensemble models consistently provided higher robustness across varying water chemistries. Hybrid and ensemble methods are increasingly viewed as state-of-the-art in NF-ML applications. They combine the predictive accuracy of deep models with the interpretability of ensemble trees, offering both performance and mechanistic insights.

Hybrid and ensemble frameworks clearly represent a methodological frontier for NF research, yet their complexity also introduces challenges. The combination of multiple models can obscure error sources and make it difficult to diagnose why a given framework succeeds or fails under specific conditions. Moreover, the computational and implementation costs of such frameworks may limit their adoption in routine engineering practice, particularly in resource-constrained settings. To ensure that these advanced approaches translate into real-world impact, future studies should report not only accuracy metrics but also computational requirements, implementation complexity, and comparative performance against simpler baselines.

Taken together, the comparative analysis presented in [Table 3](#page-17-0) indicates that no single ML approach is universally optimal for NF applications. Tree-based ensemble models and shallow neural networks currently dominate NF research due to their strong performance with small-to-medium tabular datasets, high predictive accuracy, and, in the case of ensemble methods, favourable interpretability through feature-importance analysis. DL architectures, including recurrent and convolutional neural networks (CNNs), exhibit clear advantages in handling time-dependent fouling dynamics and imaging-based analyses, respectively; however, their application remains constrained by data availability, computational demand, and limited interpretability.

Across all algorithm families, persistent methodological challenges are evident, including reliance on limited and heterogeneous datasets, inconsistent validation strategies, insufficient treatment of data leakage, and scarce uncertainty quantification. Addressing these issues will require a shift towards benchmark-oriented evaluation practices, supported by open and standardised datasets, transparent validation protocols, and systematic uncertainty analysis. Such developments are essential for translating current algorithmic advances into robust and generalisable tools for NF membrane design, process optimisation, and plant-scale operation.

The observed performance differences among ML models in NF applications can be largely attributed to the nature of NF processes and the structure of available datasets. Tree-based ensemble models frequently outperform linear approaches because NF performance metrics such as flux, solute rejection, and ion selectivity are governed by non-linear interactions among membrane structural parameters (e.g., pore size, surface charge, hydrophilicity), solute properties, and operating conditions. Ensemble methods effectively capture these interactions while remaining robust to multicollinearity and noise, which are common features of experimental NF datasets.

# 5. DL: ARCHITECTURES, APPLICATIONS, AND CHALLENGES

# 5.1. Introduction to DL

DL is a specialised subset of ML that employs multi-layered neural networks capable of extracting hierarchical and abstract representations from data. Unlike traditional ML algorithms, which rely heavily on engineered features, DL methods can automatically learn feature representations directly from raw or minimally processed inputs. This makes them particularly effective for handling large, complex, and non-linear datasets. For NF, DL has become increasingly relevant in modelling flux behaviour, solute rejection, fouling dynamics, and membrane material properties. NF systems are highly non-linear due to the interplay of pressure, solute chemistry, water chemistry, and membrane structure. DL's capacity to learn from such complex interactions makes it an ideal candidate for advancing predictive modelling in this field.

# 5.2. Convolutional neural networks

CNNs are designed to extract spatial features, making them effective for image-based or structurally complex data. In NF, CNNs have been employed for both material modelling and fouling analysis. Abdu et al. [\(2025\)](#page-25-0) designed a real-time hybrid NF/RO performance prediction framework for seawater desalination plants using DL. Their model, which incorporated CNN-based feature extraction alongside feed-forward layers, predicted permeate flux, salt rejection, and energy consumption with R<sup>2</sup> values above 0.95. Beyond accuracy, the framework demonstrated adaptability to variations in feedwater salinity and transmembrane pressure, making it suitable for real-time plant optimisation. The authors highlighted the potential of such CNN-enhanced systems to serve as the computational backbone of digital twins, providing operators with predictive insights and control recommendations that reduce fouling risk and energy demand.

Complementing this, [Shahouni](#page-29-0) et al. (2024) reviewed recent advances in applying AI, including CNNs, to predict membrane behaviour in water purification and desalination. They reported that CNNs are particularly effective in capturing non-linear solute–membrane interaction patterns when datasets include diverse operational conditions and solute properties. Compared with SVMs and regression methods, CNN-based frameworks consistently delivered higher accuracy and better generalisation, while emerging explainability techniques were noted as essential for improving trust in their predictions. These studies illustrate how CNN-driven DL is shifting from experimental modelling towards plant-scale deployment. By enabling accurate, real-time, and interpretable predictions, CNN frameworks represent a crucial step towards fully intelligent NF/RO desalination systems.

#### 5.3. RNNs, LSTM, and GRU

RNNs and their variants, LSTM and GRU, are designed to capture temporal dependencies in sequential data. In NF, they are especially useful for modelling flux decline and fouling development over time. Niu et al. [\(2025\)](#page-29-0) applied LSTM and GRU models to predict fouling caused by DOM. Their study demonstrated that recurrent architectures achieved R<sup>2</sup> . 0.93, outperforming ANN and RF models. LSTM's 'memory cells' allowed it to capture long-term fouling trends that regression and RF models missed. The GRU model, while slightly less accurate, offered computational efficiency, making it suitable for real-time applications. The authors concluded that RNNs provide an effective tool for predicting time-dependent NF performance, especially for fouling control. RNN-based models add unique value in NF research because fouling processes are inherently dynamic. Their ability to retain memory of prior states aligns with the physical reality of NF, where fouling accumulates progressively.

#### 5.4. Multimodal and hybrid DL approaches

As NF applications expand, researchers increasingly recognise that single-source datasets are insufficient to capture the complex interplay of factors governing membrane performance. Multimodal DL, the integration of heterogeneous data sources such as operational parameters, solute descriptors, and membrane structural properties has emerged as a promising direction. By combining complementary modalities, models can learn richer representations that mirror the multidimensional nature of NF processes. [Shahouni](#page-29-0) et al. (2024) highlighted the promise of multimodal approaches in their review of AI for water purification. They argued that integrating physicochemical descriptors of solutes with operational data enables models to capture both intrinsic rejection mechanisms (e.g., size exclusion, electrostatic interactions) and external influences (e.g., pH, ionic strength).

Beyond multimodal data fusion, hybrid DL frameworks combine different neural architectures or integrate DL with ensemble methods to achieve both accuracy and interpretability. Xiao et al. [\(2025\)](#page-30-0) exemplified this trend with their gradient-boosted convolutional neural network (GBCNN), where CNN layers captured non-linear solute–membrane interactions and the boosting stage provided interpretability through feature importance rankings. The GBCNN outperformed standalone CNN and RF models, confirming the added value of hybridisation.

Multimodal and hybrid DL approaches mark a shift from narrow, single-purpose models towards integrated frameworks capable of supporting both scientific discovery and operational optimisation. By fusing multiple data sources and combining architectures, these models can address the twin challenges of non-linear complexity and practical interpretability. The continued adoption of such approaches is likely to define the next generation of NF DL research, linking molecular-level descriptors with plant-scale operational intelligence.

#### 5.5. Data scarcity and augmentation strategies

One of the key barriers to applying DL in NF is the limited dataset size. NF experiments are costly and time-consuming, leading to small and fragmented datasets. DL models, however, typically require large datasets to avoid overfitting. [Zhang](#page-30-0) et al. [\(2021\)](#page-30-0) addressed this problem by using data augmentation in their CNN-inspired representation learning model. By rationally distorting monomer structures and artificially expanding the dataset, they trained a DL model that achieved high accuracy despite limited experimental data. This demonstrated that augmentation can partially compensate for data scarcity in NF. Although not yet fully explored in NF, some studies (Cao [et al.](#page-26-0) 2024) have suggested that transfer learning pre-training DL models on large general datasets and fine-tuning them for NF applications could be an effective strategy to overcome small datasets.

#### 5.6. Challenges of DL in NF

Although DL has shown remarkable promise for advancing NF research, its application remains constrained by several critical challenges. Perhaps the most significant limitation is data scarcity. Unlike fields such as computer vision or natural language processing, which benefit from massive datasets, NF studies are generally based on small experimental datasets due to the high cost, time, and resource demands of laboratory testing. This limitation is problematic because DL models, such as CNNs and RNNs, typically require large datasets to achieve generalisation and avoid overfitting. Data augmentation strategies can artificially expand available datasets and improve predictive performance, yet such methods cannot fully replicate the richness of diverse experimental data, leaving DL applications highly system-specific. A second challenge is the computational cost associated with training deep architectures. Unlike regression or tree-based models, which can be trained quickly on standard personal computers, CNNs and recurrent models demand significant computational resources, including high-performance GPUs. This requirement creates barriers for smaller laboratories or research groups without access to advanced computing facilities.

Another persistent concern is the black-box nature of DL. While DL models achieve high predictive accuracy, their internal decision-making processes are often difficult to interpret, limiting their acceptance in scientific and regulatory contexts. Although hybrid frameworks and explainability tools have been introduced to provide insights into variable importance and model reasoning, the broader challenge remains: DL predictions frequently lack transparent mechanistic grounding, which reduces their reliability for process design and optimisation. Finally, generalisation represents a substantial obstacle. Models trained on specific datasets often fail when applied to new feedwater chemistries, membrane materials, or operational conditions. This problem arises because DL models tend to capture dataset-specific correlations rather than universal mechanistic principles. Approaches such as transfer learning and digital twin frameworks hold promise in addressing this limitation, but practical demonstrations in NF remain scarce. The following sections illustrate these fields. Traditionally, most researchers tend to apply several ML algorithms/models then obtain and compare models' accuracy inferred by statistical parameters like regression R<sup>2</sup> and root mean square error (RMSE), towards selecting the most appropriate and accurate model. # 6. NF MEMBRANE APPLICATIONS INTEGRATED WITH ML

#### 6.1. Operational performance evaluation and prediction

From an operational point of view and for NF membrane filtering specific aqueous solution, NF membrane concerning parameters are flux determined by pressure magnitude, feed substances concentration determining rejection rate, and membrane recovery rate. These parameters are of interest to researchers to estimate and predict using various supervised ML algorithms, either using their own experimental data or by applying sets of data extracted from the literature. Practically, features of ML algorithms would be experimental data obtained for the operational parameters intended to be predicted, along with some additional influencing parameters, such as operational pressure, pH, solution temperature, and other relevant parameters.

Recently, ML prediction models have been widely applied for NF membranes performance evaluation (Ma et al. [2023;](#page-28-0) [Zhu](#page-31-0) [et al.](#page-31-0) 2023; [Masuodi](#page-28-0) et al. 2024; Wang et al. [2024a](#page-30-0); [Sutariya](#page-29-0) et al. 2025). Ma et al. [\(2023\)](#page-28-0) emphasised the relationship between structural and operational conditions of NF membrane applied for water desalination. They applied two ML algorithms: RF and XGBoost, to investigate the relationship between NF membrane structural parameters and operating conditions representing water/salt selectivity. Specifically, two key structural parameters of polyamide NF membranes (pore radius and zeta potential) and two operational parameters (applied pressure and feed concentration) were extracted from 100 journal publications (forming 360 data point) and analysed for their association with water/salt separation performance. The models learned from these variables to evaluate their influence on selectivity. Predicted results revealed that structural parameters had a greater impact on water/salt selectivity than operating conditions, with the degree of influence varying according to salt type. For symmetrical salts, separation was predominantly governed by size sieving, whereas asymmetrical salts were mainly influenced by Donnan exclusion. Partial dependence plot analysis further elucidated structure/performance relationships between pore radius, zeta potential, and variations in water/salt selectivity. Masuodi [et al.](#page-28-0) [\(2024\)](#page-28-0) used an ML model to evaluate micropollutant removal efficiency of NF and RO membranes. In their study, they evaluated the predictive performance of three advanced ML models: ANN, RF, and XGBoost on micropollutant removal efficiency by NF and RO from water. Their investigation specifically explored the impact of MW and compound size influence on removal rates. Employing an extensive dataset, the ANN model was developed with a four-layer architecture comprising 128, 64, 32, and 32 nodes, trained over 2,500 epochs. The ANN model outperformed the other two models, achieving an (R<sup>2</sup> : 0.9795), demonstrating its precision in capturing the variability inherent in RO and NF processes. The RF model (R<sup>2</sup> : 0.9658) and the XGBoost model (R<sup>2</sup> : 0.9447) also showed strong predictive capabilities, although they were slightly less accurate than the ANN. These findings highlight the potential of ML models, particularly ANNs, to optimise water substances removal techniques in RO and NF processes, providing valuable insights for enhancing the efficiency of these technologies. Based on NF membranes synthesised conditions, [Sutariya](#page-29-0) et al. (2025) applied an ML-assisted performance prediction model to overcome the complexity of process permutation occurrence due to variables multitude involvement. According to their study, NF membranes key parameters (flux, rejection, and permeability) performance prediction required developing a conventional multiple linear regression and ML models (RF and multi-layer perceptron (MLP)). The input variables used to develop the predictive models include the concentration of piperazine (PIP) (0.01–2 wt%), trimesoylent chloride (TMC) (0.05–0.15 wt%), sodium lauryl sulfate (SLS) (0–10 mM), and reaction time (5–1,200 s). While the traditional regression model failed to accurately predict membrane performance, the RF model demonstrated superior predictive accuracy for permeability and NaCl rejection, achieving R<sup>2</sup> values of 0.9806 and 0.9812 for training, and 0.9669 and 0.9082 for testing, respectively. For Na2SO4 rejection, the MLP model outperformed the RF model, with R<sup>2</sup> values of 0.9972 for testing and 0.9844 for training. The optimal membrane performance was observed with permeance ranging from 16 to 20 LMH/bar and selectivity between NaCl and Na2SO4 exceeding 4,000. This performance was achieved under specific synthesis conditions: lower PIP concentrations (0.05–0.1 wt%), intermediate TMC concentrations (0.1 wt%), low SLS concentrations (1 mM), and shorter reaction times (5–30 s). The best-performing ML models provided valuable insights into the relative importance of these variables in determining membrane performance. This facilitates a deeper understanding of the correlation between model predictions and the fundamental principles of membrane fabrication processes, aiding in the design of high-performance NF membranes. Zhu et al. [\(2023\)](#page-31-0) applied four traditional learning algorithms (MLR, SVM, ANN, kNN) and four ensemble learning algorithms (RF, GBDT, XGBoost, LightGBM) to predict contaminant removal efficiency in the NF membrane process. Their results indicate that ensemble models significantly outperformed traditional models in predictive accuracy. Additionally, an effective balance between accuracy and interpretability for the ensemble models through a robust model interpretation approach could be achieved. Feature selection was performed using the model interpretation method to identify the most influential variables affecting contaminant removal rates. Accordingly, the ensemble models retrained with the selected features demonstrated exceptional predictive performance, achieving adjusted R<sup>2</sup> values between 92.4 and 99.5%. Finally, the study revealed that MWCO of the membrane, McGowan volume of the solute (V), and MW of the compounds emerged as the most critical factors influencing contaminant removal in NF and RO processes. In general, ML-based models emerged as promising and further accurate approaches towards better performance evaluation and prediction modelling for NF membranes. Despite the strong predictive performance reported in these operational studies, several critical issues persist. Most models are trained on datasets compiled from either individual laboratories or literature sources that differ in analytical methods, membrane characterisation protocols, and reporting standards, which may introduce hidden biases. In addition, long-term stability, seasonal variability, and rare events are seldom represented in the training data, limiting the reliability of predictions for real plant operation. As a result, MLbased operational models for NF should currently be viewed as decision-support tools that complement, rather than replace, conventional engineering judgement and safety margins.

#### 6.2. Fabrication parameters optimisation

Numerous techniques have been developed to enhance NF membrane performance. These methods aim to boost separation capabilities, raise productivity and selectivity, chemical resistance, improve thermal stability, lower rejection efficacy, and progress in the membrane lifetime, and give antifouling features [\(Samavati](#page-29-0) et al. 2023; Liu [et al.](#page-28-0) 2024). Among innovative fabrication, manufacturing, and modification procedures with a high fouling propensity and performance increase have been the subject of numerous research. This includes exploring fabrication and modification methods, including interfacial polymerisation (IP), grafting polymerisation (GP), hollow fibre, electrospun nanofibrous membranes (ENMs), phase inversion (PI), cross-linking (CL), layer-by-layer (LbL) technology, plasma polymerisation (PP), UV/photo grafting technique, plasma treatment, blending, and coating are some of the categories into which these techniques fall (An [et al.](#page-26-0) 2023; [Behroozi](#page-26-0) [et al.](#page-26-0) 2023; [Al Kholif](#page-26-0) et al. 2024). Practically, fabrication parameters are commonly including polymer type, monomer, catalyst concentrations, reaction time, and others [\(Bahreini](#page-26-0) et al. 2024). IP was applied by [Usman](#page-30-0) et al. (2024) to fabricate three different NF membrane using three different cross-linkers for a combined application of saline water desalination and micropollutants removal. They used fabrication parameters including the concentration of PIP (0.01–2 wt%), the concentration of trimesoylchloride (TMC) (0.05–0.15 wt%), the concentration of SLS (0–10 mM), and reaction time (5–1,200 s) as input while having flux (before and after chlorination process) and rejection of sodium chloride and sodium sulphate as outputs. They explored the influence of different monomers on both structural and functional membranes features by applying Martin Gaussian Process Regression (MGPR) ML model, achieving as high as 99% accuracy. The database and information available for various types of polymers, especially those that have not been used or applied could be useful to train ML models towards selecting the best fit polymer for membrane fabrication. Wang et al. [\(2024a\)](#page-30-0) introduced an ML and explainable-AI framework for predicting LBL NF membrane performance and identifying high-potential polymer candidates. Four ML models were evaluated, with XGBoost achieving the highest accuracy (R<sup>2</sup> <sup>¼</sup> 0.99 for permeability, 0.80 for selectivity). Applying SHAP, their study revealed the influence of fabrication parameters and polymer structures, represented using Morgan fingerprints. Two reference fingerprints capturing beneficial atomic groups were used to screen 204 polymers from the Poly-info database, yielding 23 promising candidates for LBL NF membrane fabrication. OSN enables efficient separation of solvent–solute mixtures (200–1,000 Da) at mild temperatures without phase transitions, yet its industrial uptake is hindered by limited long-term stability data and a lack of predictive design tools (Gu et al. [2025\)](#page-27-0). [Goebel](#page-27-0) et al. (2020) extended an automated membranespecific modelling framework, previously applied to solvent flux prediction, to quantify solute rejection in pure and mixed solvents. Using a PuraMem S600 membrane, they measured solute rejection across six solvents, and model candidates were automatically derived and validated. In their study, mixed solvent performance was further evaluated via membrane rejection maps using PuraMem 280 data. Their models achieved ,10% deviation between measured and predicted rejection for most cases, with comparable accuracy in mixed solvents. Their approach offers a robust, data-driven tool for OSN process design, accelerating its deployment in chemical separations. It is worth noting that attempts to develop ML models based on NF membrane fabrication parameters are yet to be established or found in literature.

The reviewed work on fabrication optimisation underscores the promise of ML for accelerating membrane development; however, significant gaps remain. Most studies focus on relatively narrow regions of fabrication space and on a limited number of polymer systems, restricting the ability of models to extrapolate to genuinely novel chemistries or structures. Moreover, experimental datasets are rarely designed with ML in mind, leading to uneven coverage of key variables and difficulties in disentangling correlated effects. Until larger, systematically designed fabrication datasets become available for NF, MLguided optimisation should be interpreted as a tool for hypothesis generation and local refinement rather than as a fully predictive design engine.

#### 6.3. Fouling monitoring and cleaning schemes

The seriousness of the membrane fouling issue attracted the attention of many researchers to benefit from ML evolution towards studying and predicting concerning and influencing parameters in mitigation and cleaning processes. Practically, fouling is usually diagnosed by observing flux decline and selectivity deterioration represented by rejection reduction with time [\(Al Kholif](#page-26-0) et al. 2024; [Fujioka](#page-27-0) et al. 2024). These parameters were targeted as feature inputs for ML algorithms in addition to other influencing parameters, including pH, temperature, and feedwater quality, while the outputs of such models included: recovery rate, permeate quality represented by TDS level, cake layer thickness, permeate flux, and fouling resistance (Wang et al. [2024c](#page-30-0); [Zheng](#page-31-0) et al. 2024b). Generally, identifying early indicators of fouling and optimising cleaning schedules, data-driven models like NN, SVM, and RF have been successfully used to analyse large volumes of process data (e.g., transmembrane pressure, permeate flux, conductivity, and chemical dosage) ([Ignacz](#page-27-0) et al. 2025; [Liang](#page-28-0) et al. 2025). Additionally, ML and AI-based systems can facilitate adaptive process control, which enhances membrane lifespan and lowers energy and chemical usage by automatically modifying operating parameters to reduce fouling in real time.

Furthermore, to increase robustness and interpretability as well as closing the gap between theory and practice in NF system monitoring, hybrid models that combine physics-based techniques with ML algorithms are being created more frequently (Figure 5) [\(Habieeb](#page-27-0) et al. 2023). Smart, AI-assisted membrane systems are a major trend in next-generation water treatment technologies, as these advancements mark a change in NF operations from reactive to predictive maintenance.

Flux decline in NF/RO membrane processes, where membranes were fouled by synthesised humic acid, was investigated by Park et al. [\(2019\)](#page-29-0). In their study, a CNN was developed to model membrane fouling in NF an RO using in situ optical coherence tomography (OCT) images. Trained on 13,708 high-resolution fouling layer images, the CNN simulated both organic fouling growth and flux decline, producing accurate 2D and 3D reconstructions. Compared with conventional mathematical models, their model achieved superior accuracy (R<sup>2</sup> <sup>¼</sup> 0.99, RMSE <sup>¼</sup> 2.82 <sup>μ</sup>m for fouling growth; <sup>R</sup><sup>2</sup> <sup>¼</sup> 0.99, RMSE <sup>¼</sup> 0.30 L m<sup>2</sup> h<sup>1</sup> for flux decline), demonstrating the potential of data-driven modelling as a robust alternative for predicting fouling and performance loss in high-pressure membrane systems. Technically, membrane fouling forecasting enables proactive strategies to sustain filtration performance. Shim et al. [\(2021\)](#page-29-0) developed an LSTM model to predict permeate flux and fouling growth in NF system filtering NOM. Laboratory experiments using four NOMs foulants, namely, humic acid, bovine serum albumin, sodium alginate, and tannic acid, were conducted with real-time 2D OCT capturing cake layer formation. The LSTM, trained on experimental data, achieved RMSE below 1 L m<sup>2</sup> h<sup>1</sup> for flux and 10 μm for layer thickness. Their study demonstrates that obtaining high prediction accuracy was attributed to conducting the prediction for a mixture of NOM filtration rather than filtering a single solution which in turn positively affects the training information. Overall, ML-based fouling monitoring and cleaning optimisation frameworks provide clear advantages over purely empirical or rule-based approaches; nevertheless, their deployment faces several challenges. Many models are trained on relatively short experimental campaigns and do not account for long-term membrane ageing, changes in feedwater composition or sensor drift, all of which are common in practice. Furthermore, the lack of standardised performance indicators and benchmark datasets hampers objective comparison between competing approaches. To move from promising prototypes to robust operational tools, future studies will need to integrate ML with mechanistic fouling understanding, perform long-term validation in full-scale systems and explicitly consider the reliability and maintainability of sensing infrastructure.

# 7. DATA-DRIVEN MODELLING VS. MECHANISTIC AND CONVENTIONAL MODELLING IN NF

#### 7.1. Advantages of ML models over conventional models

NF membranes have long been described by conventional mechanistic models, including the SDM, Spiegler–Kedem model, and extended Nernst–Planck equations. These models offer conceptual clarity and interpretability because they are based on thermodynamic ([Bowen & Mohammad 1998](#page-26-0); [Geraldes & Afonso 2007\)](#page-27-0). However, their accuracy is limited when applied to complicated or real-world systems since they frequently rely on simplifying assumptions (e.g., homogeneous membrane characteristics, steady-state circumstances, low concentration polarisation). Furthermore, mechanistic models usually necessitate an accurate understanding of system parameters, which can change over time due to fouling or aging and are challenging to assess in situ. Examples of these factors include membrane transport coefficients, solute diffusivity, and charge interactions. ML models, on the other hand, are excellent at extracting non-linear relationships and patterns from both historical and current data without the need for pre-existing physical assumptions (Figure 6). Even when mechanistic models are unsuccessful, methods such as ANNs, SVR, and ensemble learning can accurately forecast membrane performance (e.g., rejection rate, permeate flux) under a variety of circumstances [\(Masuodi](#page-28-0) et al. 2024; Sun [et al.](#page-29-0) 2025). Additionally, ML models are particularly useful in dynamic operating situations because they can be updated with fresh data on a regular basis, allowing for adaptive learning and real-time decision assistance. ML models are a strong supplement or even a replacement for traditional modelling techniques in NF membrane applications because of their predictive power, data flexibility, and decreased reliance on system-specific constants, despite their potential lack of direct interpretability (Figure 6).

# 7.2. Data quality and availability

Data quality and availability are critical factors in the success of a ML model, often having a far greater impact on performance than other aspects such as the choice of model architecture or the specific training procedures used. Despite this, much of the discourse outside the scientific community, especially in mainstream media, tends to focus on the development of cutting-edge ML models, such as the advent of transformers, the rapid growth of large language models, and other technological advancements. This is largely because discussions about the 'AI' itself are seen as more captivating than the more nuanced topic of the data that fuels these models. However, this can lead to the misconception that sophisticated models can compensate for poor data practices, which is far from the case. Several challenges related to data quality were pointed out in several studies, including issues with data integrity ([Gudivada](#page-27-0) et al. 2017), concerns regarding the data lifecycle ([Polyzotis](#page-29-0) et al. [2018\)](#page-29-0), the integration with ML-OPS [\(Gudivada](#page-27-0) et al. 2017), and model management practices ([Schelter](#page-29-0) et al. 2015). Additionally, some studies have proposed frameworks for assessing data quality ([Gupta](#page-27-0) et al. 2021), automating data quality verification processes [\(Schelter](#page-29-0) et al. 2018), and methodologies for summarising dataset quality through tools like datasheets [\(Crawford 2021](#page-26-0)), nutritional labels ([Stoyanovich & Howe 2019](#page-29-0)), and data cards ([Tagliabue](#page-29-0) et al. 2021).

Historically, both academic research and industry efforts have concentrated on advancing or creating new ML models, often overlooking data challenges that extend beyond basic cleaning or pre-processing. However, the practical performance of AI-driven systems has demonstrated that it is ultimately constrained by the quality of the training data used ([Polyzotis](#page-29-0) et al. [2019\)](#page-29-0). Additionally, data typically have long lifecycles and are not confined to a single task; instead, they can be continuously utilised to refine and develop new models for solving emerging problems. This realisation has prompted a shift in research emphasis, moving from a model-centric to a data-centric approach in AI system development ([Ré 2021\)](#page-29-0). Contemporary AI applications demand vast amounts of training and testing data. This necessity presents significant challenges not only in ensuring the availability of sufficient data but also in maintaining its quality. For instance, incomplete, inaccurate, or unsuitable data can result in unreliable models that generate poor outcomes. Reliable AI systems depend on high-quality data across multiple dimensions, including accuracy, completeness, and consistency. [Mohammed](#page-29-0) et al. (2025) examined the effect of six key data quality dimensions on ML performance across three distinct tasks: classification, regression, and clustering. Their study evaluated 19 commonly used ML algorithms on 10 diverse datasets, considering various scenarios of both high and low data quality through the constructing and investigating of three specific scenarios with three approaches: classification, regression, and clustering. The study revealed that data consistency and uniqueness had low effects while completeness and feature accuracy have high effects on the three scenarios accordingly.

# 8. FUTURE PROSPECTS AND CONCLUSIONS

The integration of ML and AI has established a new paradigm in NF membranes applications, moving predictive capabilities beyond the constraints of classical mechanistic modelling. As evidenced by the robust accuracy scored by ML modelling complex phenomena, ML is not merely a tool for pattern recognition; it is a catalyst for data-driven scientific discovery. However, translating such success into widespread industrial scalability and reliability requires acknowledging and systematically addressing persistent methodological bottlenecks. The following aspects are important to point out:

Technological implications and industrial scalability: the most significant technological implication of ML in NF is the realisation of proactive, adaptive process control. Future systems will integrate real-time sensor data with high accuracy predictive models (e.g., CNNs and LSTM) to create Digital Twins, enabling autonomous decision-making. This would involve:

- Autonomous optimisation: employing RL agents trained in simulated environments to continuously optimise operating parameters (pressure, crossflow velocity, feed concentration, and cleaning frequency) in real-time to maintain flux and maximise recovery, thereby reducing human intervention and energy costs.
- Predictive maintenance: utilising DL time-series models to predict fouling events days or weeks in advance, allowing for cleaning protocols to initiate proactively, thus extending membrane lifespan.
- Inverse design: leveraging generative ML models to identify the optimal chemical structures and process conditions for NF membranes tailored to specific source waters (e.g., high-salinity brine, complex industrial effluent), accelerating the design process and breaking the current permeability–selectivity trade-off.

<span id="page-25-0"></span>Critical reflection of data, generalisation, and trust: the true limitations of the current ML-NF literature are not algorithmic but rather methodological, centering on data practices as follows:

- Data scarcity and heterogeneity: the reliance on small, single-laboratory datasets severely limits the generalisation of models. Future research must prioritise open science initiatives, creating large, standardised, multi-site datasets that capture operational, chemical, and structural heterogeneity, supported by unified data reporting protocols.
- Model generalisation (robustness): current models often capture system-specific correlations, failing when transferred to unseen facilities. To achieve industrial reliability, research must focus on physics-informed ML and hybrid MLmechanistic frameworks. By embedding known transport equations (e.g., Nerst–Planck) into neural network loss functions, models can be constrained by physical laws, dramatically improving their robustness and external validity across different feedwater compositions.
- Industrial scalability and trust: Industrial adoption is hindered by the 'black-box' nature of complex models. Future efforts must mandate or encourage the use of explainable AI (XAI) as a standard practice, not an afterthought. XAI tools must be refined to translate statistical feature importance (e.g., SHAP values) directly, into clear mechanistic causality that researchers/engineers and regulators can trust, such as quantifying the exact contribution of the membrane's zeta potential versus the feed's pH on divalent ion rejection.

In summary, ML is set to revolutionise NF from laboratory synthesis to field operation. The technological implications point towards highly efficient, autonomous, and specialised NF systems. However, the researchers must prioritise solving the foundational problems of data standardisation and model transparency to ensure that these sophisticated tools successfully transition from promising research concepts to reliable, scalable solutions contributing meaningfully to global water treatment and security.
